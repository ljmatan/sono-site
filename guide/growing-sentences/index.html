Usage guide

From one word to sentences

The person uses single words: how do you help them join two or three, and what does Sono say when they do? Here is what research on combining symbols suggests, and how to do it with the sentence strip and sentence starters.

Not medical advice. This guide shares general information and everyday experience. It is not medical, therapeutic or diagnostic advice. For decisions about your child, talk to their doctor, speech and language therapist or another qualified professional.

On this page

  1. Add one word to theirs
  2. Show two words together
  3. The sentence strip and sentence starters
  4. Short messages are real messages
  5. What Sono says when Speak is tapped

Add one word to theirs # — Copy link to this section

When the person says something with a single symbol, answer it, then show the same message with one more word. They tap JUICE; you tap WANT, JUICE while you say "You want juice!" This is called expanding. It isn't a correction and nobody has to copy it: you show what the next step looks like, using their own idea.

AssistiveWare's guide for communication partners recommends exactly this, with the example of a person who says MORE and a partner who models WANT + MORE. Researchers who followed four children aged 9 to 13 as they began to build sentences on talking devices, devices that speak the symbols they tap, describe familiar adults rephrasing the children's messages and building sentences together with them, and conclude that the communication partner plays a crucial part in how their language grows. ASHA, the American Speech-Language-Hearing Association, notes that grammar can be especially hard to learn when someone communicates with symbols, and that people can be taught to begin combining words into sentences.

How to do it in Sono

  1. First answer the message, or give them what they asked for. It worked, and that comes first.
  2. Then model their word plus one. For example:
    • They tap JUICE. You say: "You want juice!" You tap: WANT, JUICE.
    • They tap BALL. You say: "A big ball!" You tap: BIG, BALL.
    • They tap GO. You say: "Let's go outside!" You tap: GO, OUTSIDE.
  3. Speak in full, natural sentences, and tap only the key words.
  4. One word more is enough: the idea is to stay just one step ahead.
  5. Don't ask them to repeat it. If they do, wonderful; if not, there will be another chance soon.

Sources

  • AssistiveWare — Communication partner skills for AAC learners
  • Savaldi-Harussi, Lustigman & Soto (2019) — The emergence of clause construction in children who use SGDs (speech-generating devices). Augmentative and Alternative Communication 35(2)
  • ASHA — Augmentative and Alternative Communication (Practice Portal)

Show two words together # — Copy link to this section

Joining words can be shown the same way single words are: by modelling, now two words at a time. In a study with five preschool children, an adult pointed to two symbols on the child's own board or device during play and then said the whole sentence out loud. Four of the five learned to combine symbols themselves, kept doing it over time and used the combinations in new games; the fifth showed no consistent gains. In another study, parents learned a strategy to use while reading books with their children: all of them learned it, and all of the children produced more messages of two or more symbols.

Every person is different, and a speech and language therapist can help you choose the first word pairs.

How to do it in Sono

  1. Pick two or three pairs for one routine this week, made of words the person already knows. For example:
    • Snack: WANT + COOKIE, MORE + JUICE.
    • Play: MORE + BUBBLES, PUSH + SWING, BIG + BALL.
    • Going out: GO + OUTSIDE, GO + HOME.
  2. Say the full sentence and tap the two words. For example:
    • You say: "Do you want more bubbles?" You tap: MORE, BUBBLES.
  3. In the Advanced and Menu layouts, double-tap each word to put both on the sentence strip, then tap Speak: Sono says the pair as one message. In the Simple layout, tap the two words one after the other.
  4. Books are a good place to practise. Talk about the pictures two words at a time. For example:
    • You say: "Look, the cat is sleeping." You tap: CAT, SLEEP.
  5. Keep the same pairs for a while, so the person sees them many times, and keep modelling single words too.

Sources

  • Binger & Light (2007) — The effect of aided AAC modeling on the expression of multi-symbol messages by preschoolers who use AAC. Augmentative and Alternative Communication 23(1)
  • Binger, Kent-Walsh, Berens, Del Campo & Rivera (2008) — Teaching Latino parents to support the multi-symbol message productions of their children who require AAC. Augmentative and Alternative Communication 24(4)

The sentence strip and sentence starters # — Copy link to this section

Sono has two tools for joining words.

The sentence strip, in the Advanced and Menu layouts, collects words until Speak is tapped. With the default Tap Behavior, one tap says a word and a double tap adds it to the strip; if double-tapping is hard, Caretaker Settings > Board Display > Tap Behavior can make a single tap add it. Building a sentence has the details.

Sentence starters sit on the Start tab in the Advanced and Simple layouts. A starter such as "I want…" says its words and, in the Advanced layout, puts them on the strip, so one more word finishes the sentence. Carole Zangari, a professor of speech-language pathology, points out that ready-made phrases can save effort and speed up a conversation, and that full sentences and questions may help beginners learn language. She adds that they shouldn't make up most of what a person can say: that would be far too limiting and keep them from expressing their own thoughts.

How to do it in Sono

  1. Start with single words and two-word models. Bring in a starter when the person often says the same kind of thing, such as asking for something.
  2. Model it in the Advanced layout. Open the Start tab and tap "I want…": Sono says "I want" and opens Basics. Open Food, double-tap JUICE, then tap Speak, and Sono says "I want juice".
  3. In the Simple layout, which has no strip, the starter is spoken on its own, and the next word follows with its own tap.
  4. The Menu layout has no Start tile. There, model WANT and a word on the sentence strip instead.
  5. Under Caretaker Settings > Sentence Starters, switch off the starters you don't use yet, and add your own with Add starter.
  6. Keep single words within reach. JUICE on its own should still get the person juice.

Sources

  • PrAACtical AAC (Zangari, 2015) — On full sentences and questions in AAC systems

Short messages are real messages # — Copy link to this section

WANT + JUICE is a complete message, even without "I" and "please". Asking for the whole sentence, "Say: I want juice, please", turns a message that already worked into a test, and every extra word costs time and effort.

Researchers who studied four children using talking devices note that children who communicate with symbols often leave out the small grammar words, and suggest one reason: the wish to communicate quickly and efficiently can favour short messages. Carole Zangari notes that people for whom reaching the symbols is physically hard weigh whether a moment is worth the physical and mental energy of a full answer, and often opt out, not because they are lazy or unwilling but to save energy. AssistiveWare's guide to being a respectful communication partner asks partners to accept communication in whatever form or combination the person chooses, and to wait as long as it takes.

What to do

  1. Answer the message they gave you. Then, if you like, model a slightly longer version, as in Add one word to theirs.
  2. Don't ask for "the whole sentence" or for "please" before you respond.
  3. Wait while they build. Finding the second word can take a long time: count silently to ten or more, and don't jump in with prompts or help, as AssistiveWare's guide for communication partners advises. See also Give time, then answer every attempt.
  4. Keep your hands off the screen while they build: don't finish the sentence or tap Speak for them.
  5. Words in an unusual order still count. Answer what they meant, and model the usual order once, cheerfully.

Sources

  • Savaldi-Harussi, Lustigman & Soto (2019) — The emergence of clause construction in children who use SGDs (speech-generating devices). Augmentative and Alternative Communication 35(2)
  • PrAACtical AAC (Zangari, 2015) — On full sentences and questions in AAC systems
  • AssistiveWare — How to be a respectful communication partner
  • AssistiveWare — Communication partner skills for AAC learners

What Sono says when Speak is tapped # — Copy link to this section

When Speak is tapped, Sono reads the words on the sentence strip in the order they were added, as one sentence, with the chosen voice. In English, German, Italian, French, Spanish, Finnish and Danish, it doesn't add, guess or reorder anything: the words are said as they appear on the symbols. WANT + JUICE is spoken as "want juice", and "I want…" from the Start tab followed by JUICE as "I want juice".

In Croatian, Bosnian, Serbian, Montenegrin, Czech, Polish and Slovak, Sono also adjusts word endings so the words fit together: the ending a noun needs after a verb, or the verb form that goes with the person doing it. Now and then it adds or moves a small word, such as a preposition, for the same reason.

After speaking, a banner shows the sentence for a few seconds, the sentence is saved to Caretaker Settings > Message History and the strip clears. Speaking a sentence has the details.

Good to know

  • A recording made in Customise Symbols plays when that symbol is tapped on its own, but Speak always reads the whole sentence with the chosen voice.
  • Until Speak is tapped, the sentence can still change: press and hold a word on the strip to remove just that word, or tap Delete to remove the last one.
  • Tap a word on the strip to see the sentence full-screen with a large Speak button, handy for showing it to someone across the table.
  • Quick phrases (the lightning bolt) are spoken straight away and don't touch the sentence.

Reading Message History in the evening shows which combinations the person is building, and which pairs to model next.
