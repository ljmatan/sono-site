Usage guide

Exploring by tapping

The person taps every symbol in sight, presses the same one over and over, or plays with the device: a sign that they are exploring. Here is why professionals see exploring as part of learning, how to answer it, and what to do in Sono when it gets loud or the person wants to leave the app.

Not medical advice. This guide shares general information and everyday experience. It is not medical, therapeutic or diagnostic advice. For decisions about your child, talk to their doctor, speech and language therapist or another qualified professional.

On this page

  1. Exploring is part of learning
  2. Answer taps as if they were meant
  3. Model the words they land on
  4. Keeping the sound friendly for everyone
  5. Keeping Sono for talking

Exploring is part of learning # — Copy link to this section

Babies babble for months before their first words: they play with sounds, hear them and slowly find out what they do. Someone new to a talking board often does the same with symbols. They tap everything, or press one word twenty times in a row.

Deanna Wagner, a speech and language therapist writing for the Cerebral Palsy Foundation, notes that people sometimes worry about this period of exploring, and that it is part of normal development. A guide by AssistiveWare, a company that makes communication apps, compares repeated tapping to babbling and calls it a chance to explore words and where they are: finding a word becomes automatic only after many repetitions. Carole Zangari, a professor of speech-language pathology who blogs about augmentative and alternative communication, warns that stopping the repetition shuts down a tool for learning language.

Repeated tapping can mean other things too: a real message, excitement, or comfort from the sound and the feel of the tap. Every person is different, and a speech and language therapist can help you understand what you see.

What to do

  1. Let it happen. Don't take the device away or say "stop playing with it".
  2. Make time for free exploring every day. Zangari describes giving some learners five to ten minutes each morning and afternoon to explore, with no prompting and no correction.
  3. Keep the board the same from day to day. Once a Board Layout and a Grid Size work, leave them: changing either moves the symbols to new places, and the person has to find their words again.
  4. Sit nearby and join in now and then, as the next articles describe.
  5. In the evening, look at Recents, a tab in the Advanced and Simple layouts and part of Caretaker Settings > Message History in every layout. It shows the last 30 different symbols tapped, yours included.

Sources

  • Cerebral Palsy Foundation (Wagner, 2020) — Babbling and using AAC for the first time
  • AssistiveWare (Zisk & Hartmann) — Understanding why AAC users repeatedly tap buttons and practical strategies for support
  • PrAACtical AAC (Zangari, 2015) — Stimming or learning? Considerations for kids who repeat themselves with AAC

Answer taps as if they were meant # — Copy link to this section

You can't always know whether a tap was meant. Answering as if it was is how the person finds out that symbols make things happen.

AssistiveWare's guide for communication partners puts it simply: treat the person's communication as meaningful, and don't assume that what you don't understand is an accident. Its guide on repeated tapping adds: when in doubt, guess out loud what the person might mean, and make a new guess if they show you that you were wrong. Deanna Wagner of the Cerebral Palsy Foundation observes that the more you respond, the more the person will use words that are interesting and powerful.

How to do it in Sono

  1. Say the word back and react to it, as you would to a first spoken word. For example:
    • They tap COOKIE. You say: "A cookie? You want a cookie!" and, if you can, give one.
    • They tap BUBBLES. You say: "Bubbles? Let's blow bubbles!" and get them out.
  2. If you can't do what the word asks, say so honestly. For example:
    • They tap OUTSIDE at bedtime. You say: "Outside? Not now, it's night. Tomorrow!" You tap: TOMORROW.
  3. Don't correct a tap that doesn't fit the moment. Answer it, then wait to see what they do next.
  4. Watch the person, not only the screen: a look, a smile or a pushed-away hand tells you whether your guess was right. AssistiveWare's guide to being a respectful communication partner also asks partners to watch the person's face rather than the device.
  5. Words like HURT, HELP and STOP always deserve a real answer, even in the middle of a tapping game.

Sources

  • AssistiveWare — Communication partner skills for AAC learners
  • AssistiveWare (Zisk & Hartmann) — Understanding why AAC users repeatedly tap buttons and practical strategies for support
  • Cerebral Palsy Foundation (Wagner, 2020) — Babbling and using AAC for the first time
  • AssistiveWare — How to be a respectful communication partner

Model the words they land on # — Copy link to this section

Every tap hands you a topic the person chose. Use it: say something about that word, and tap one or two symbols as you talk. This is modelling, starting from their word instead of yours.

AssistiveWare's guide on repeated tapping recommends answering a word the person repeats with a related comment or question: it shows you heard them, and it adds language. Carole Zangari suggests using the board yourself and making a brief interaction around what they said. If the person keeps landing on the same symbol, offer something new that belongs with it: a describing word, an action, a related topic.

How to do it in Sono

  1. Add a word or two to theirs. For example:
    • They tap DOG. You say: "A big dog!" You tap: BIG, DOG.
    • They tap CAR again and again. You say: "The car goes fast!" You tap: CAR, GO, FAST.
    • They tap RAIN. You say: "Rain makes everything wet." You tap: RAIN, WET.
  2. Keep it light: one comment, then let them go back to exploring. Comment rather than ask, and never "show me".
  3. If you can't find a word quickly, model the ones you can find. One word that fits is enough.
  4. In the Advanced and Menu layouts, you can double-tap your words onto the sentence strip (with the default Tap Behavior) and tap Speak, so the person hears them together. In the Simple layout, tap them one after the other.
  5. Bring their favourite word back during the day. If CAR is today's word, model it in the car, at the window and with toy cars.

Sources

  • AssistiveWare (Zisk & Hartmann) — Understanding why AAC users repeatedly tap buttons and practical strategies for support
  • PrAACtical AAC (Zangari, 2015) — Stimming or learning? Considerations for kids who repeat themselves with AAC

Keeping the sound friendly for everyone # — Copy link to this section

A voice that says CAR forty times in a row can be hard on brothers and sisters, a classroom or a quiet waiting room. Still, the advice is firm. AssistiveWare's guide on repeated tapping says never to take the device away, mute it or turn it down without the person's consent: that takes away their ability to communicate and can cause real distress, and trying to keep others from hearing the repetition works against the person. Carole Zangari agrees: switching the device off, taking it away or turning it to silent is not an option.

What helps instead

  1. Look for the reason. Zangari suggests checking the surroundings: is it too loud around them, are they bored, has something changed?
  2. Change the place, not the voice. Whoever needs quiet, a sibling doing homework, for example, can move to another room for a while.
  3. Plan regular times when free tapping is especially welcome (see Exploring is part of learning), so the rest of the family knows when to expect it.
  4. Offer, never impose. The same AssistiveWare guide suggests headphones, or a volume the person can hear and others hardly can, and says these should never be enforced. Sono has no volume setting of its own: it speaks at the device's volume, and through headphones when they are connected. Make sure the person can be heard again as soon as they want to say something.
  5. Explain it to siblings and classmates: the person is exploring their words, a bit like babies babble before they talk.
  6. For places that must stay silent, add a printed board. Caretaker Settings > Print Boards makes a PDF of one category or of all of them; on Android, tap Share PDF to print it or send it on. Offer it alongside the device, not instead of it.

Sources

  • AssistiveWare (Zisk & Hartmann) — Understanding why AAC users repeatedly tap buttons and practical strategies for support
  • PrAACtical AAC (Zangari, 2015) — Stimming or learning? Considerations for kids who repeat themselves with AAC

Keeping Sono for talking # — Copy link to this section

Sono often runs on the same phone or tablet the family uses for videos and games, so some people try to leave the board or treat it as a toy. Wanting to leave can be a message too: I'm bored, I want my show, I've had enough. AbleNet, a company that makes communication devices, recommends keeping a communication device for communication only, especially at the start, so the person learns that it is their voice.

How to do it in Sono

  1. Keep Sono on the screen. Under Caretaker Settings > Board Display > Kiosk Mode, turn on Enable Kiosk Mode; on Android, Pin App to Screen then keeps the device in Sono until it's unpinned. Kiosk mode has the details.
  2. The settings are already protected: the gear asks a short addition question first.
  3. If you can, keep games and videos on another device, or give the two devices cases of a different colour or feel, so the person can tell which one is their voice.
  4. Answer the wish to leave with words. For example:
    • You say: "You want TV? Later." You tap: TV, LATER.
    • You say: "All done? Okay, we're done." You tap: ALL DONE.
    • You say: "Bored? Let's play." You tap: BORED, PLAY.
  5. If the person wants to play, the Activities tab (a tile in the Menu layout) has short games inside Sono. Play one together, and model PLAY, AGAIN, YAY and ALL DONE around it.

Never take Sono away as a consequence, and don't hand it out only as a reward. AssistiveWare's article on stimming, repeating a sound or movement because it calms or feels good, notes that removing the device is the most common punishment for stimming on it, says plainly that removing someone's device is never okay, and warns that giving it only as a reward for doing as told can also do harm. See also Keep it within reach, all day.
