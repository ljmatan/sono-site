Usage guide

The first two weeks

You've just installed Sono: what should the first two weeks look like? Here is a starting plan, from setting up the board to looking back at the end of week two; every person is different, so adapt it to them, ideally together with a speech and language therapist.

Not medical advice. This guide shares general information and everyday experience. It is not medical, therapeutic or diagnostic advice. For decisions about your child, talk to their doctor, speech and language therapist or another qualified professional.

Day one: set up a calm board # — Copy link to this section

On the first day, set Sono up so it is easy for both of you: a quiet screen, big buttons, a handful of words and a few familiar photos. Every setting can be changed later, and hidden symbols are never deleted.

How to do it in Sono

  1. Choose a layout under Caretaker Settings > Board Display > Board Layout. A new install starts in Menu, which shows big tiles, one category at a time. Simple, where one tap speaks one word, is the quietest screen to begin with. Advanced suits people who already join words. Board layouts and tap behaviour compares them.
  2. On the same screen, set Grid Size. Fewer columns mean bigger buttons: start with fewer than you think, and add columns later.
  3. Make the board small for now. Under Caretaker Settings > Categories, switch off the categories you won't need in the first weeks, such as ABCs and 123s (This week's words and routines helps you choose the words). Keep Basics and Manners on: they hold HELP, STOP, MORE, ALL DONE, YES and NO.
  4. Hide single symbols that get in the way under Caretaker Settings > Hidden Symbols. For example:
    • Basics starts with eight question words, WHAT, WHERE, WHO and five more. Hiding them for now brings HELP, STOP, MORE, NOT and ALL DONE to the top.
  5. Add two or three photos of favourite foods, toys or people in Caretaker Settings > My Symbols. Add your own photos shows how.
  6. To give a symbol a familiar voice, record one for it under Caretaker Settings > Customise Symbols.
  7. Charge the device and decide where it will live during the day, within the person's reach (Keep it within reach, all day).

A small board is a calm way to start, not the goal. As the weeks go on, bring words back a few at a time.

This week's words and routines # — Copy link to this section

Trying to model everything at once spreads you thin. AssistiveWare, a company that makes communication apps and publishes free guides about augmentative and alternative communication, advises focusing on a few words at a time, and its guide to core words suggests planning a set of words for each week or month. Its getting-started guide recommends looking at your daily routines and planning where, when and how the communication aid fits into them.

How to do it

  1. Pick three to five words for the first two weeks: two or three small words that work almost everywhere, and one or two of the person's favourites. For example:
    • MORE, STOP and ALL DONE, plus photos of their favourite snack and toy.
    • GO, HELP and LIKE, plus BUBBLES.
  2. Pick one or two routines that happen every day and that the person enjoys, or at least doesn't mind: a snack, a tickle game, bath time, getting dressed, a walk.
  3. Plan where each word fits. For example:
    • Snack: MORE when the plate is empty, ALL DONE when they push it away.
    • Tickle game: tickle, pause and model MORE; if they pull away, model STOP and stop.
    • Bubbles: blow, pause, model BUBBLES or MORE, and wait.
  4. Write the words on a note where everyone sees it, so grandparents, siblings and babysitters model the same ones. A paper copy of a category helps too: open Caretaker Settings > Print Boards, choose the category and tap Share PDF.
  5. Keep the same words for both weeks. Seeing and hearing them again and again is the point.

Choosing the first words explains the mix of words, and Through the day has ideas for each routine.

How often, and keeping notes # — Copy link to this section

How many examples a day? There is no set number. AssistiveWare's advice on modelling is to model as often as you can, because a few moments a day or a week give the person little to learn from, and it adds that any modelling is better than none. A review of studies that taught the people around a person how to support their communication found this training highly effective, and the skills taught most often included modelling and waiting expectantly.

A simple plan to start with

  1. Model each of this week's words every time its routine comes round, and whenever it fits at other moments of the day. Each example takes only a few seconds.
  2. Keep a tally of your own examples, not of the person's taps (Interest grows from seeing it used explains why). A tally on the note on the fridge works.
  3. After each example, pause and wait. Give time, then answer every attempt explains how.
  4. Keep short notes, on paper or on your phone. For example:
    • Words: which ones you modelled, and in which routine.
    • Their response: looked, reached, tapped, made a sound, pushed the device away.
    • Interest: what caught their attention, and what didn't.
  5. When something goes well, film it if you can. AssistiveWare suggests filming the moments that work, sharing them with the others who support the person, and looking at what worked so you can do it again.
  6. In the evening, look at what was tapped. Caretaker Settings > Message History shows the recent symbols in every layout and, in the Advanced and Menu layouts, the sentences spoken from the sentence strip (Message history and Recents). Your own modelling taps show up there too, so read them alongside your notes.

Sources

  • Kent-Walsh, Murza, Malani & Binger (2015) — Effects of communication partner instruction on the communication of individuals using AAC: a meta-analysis. AAC 31(4)
  • AssistiveWare — Don't give up too soon

What's normal in weeks one and two # — Copy link to this section

It's natural to hope for a first tap in the first few days. Don't worry if the first two weeks look more like this

  • Watching: the person looks at the screen, or at you, while you tap. Watching is how they learn what the board does.
  • Nothing visible yet: in a study with two preschool children), the children's use of the symbols lagged behind their understanding of them, so a person may be learning before you can see it.
  • Exploring: tapping lots of symbols, or the same one again and again. AssistiveWare, a company that makes communication apps, notes that repeated tapping can be like babbling, an early stage of learning language, and advises treating a tap as a message when you're not sure.
  • Pushing it away: that is a message too. Respect it (The right to say no), and try again at a better moment.
  • Feeling clumsy yourself: modelling can feel clumsy at first and gets easier each time.

What to do

  1. Keep going. AssistiveWare notes that teams often give up too soon, after trying a system for a few weeks, before the person has seen it used enough. Interest grows from seeing it used explains why.
  2. Give the person time to explore freely, with no questions and no tests. If the sound bothers others, AssistiveWare suggests headphones as one way to let them explore, and advises against muting the device or taking it away.
  3. Answer exploring taps as if they were meant. If they tap MORE, say "More? Okay, more!" and give more if you can.
  4. Write down anything new, however small. A first look at the screen counts.

Every person is different. If the person seems distressed by the board, or tapping looks physically hard, talk to a speech and language therapist (Every person is unique: a professional can help).

End of week two: look back and adjust # — Copy link to this section

At the end of week two, take ten minutes with your notes, ideally together with everyone who has been modelling. ASHA, the American Speech-Language-Hearing Association, notes that a communication system needs ongoing review and decisions even after it has been chosen, and AssistiveWare's guide includes the same step: review progress, reflect and make changes.

Questions to ask

  • Which words did we actually model, and in which routines?
  • Did the person look at, reach for or tap any of them?
  • Which routine worked best, and which one kept getting skipped?
  • Were the buttons easy to see and to tap?

What to adjust

  1. Keep modelling the words that are catching on.
  2. Add the next one to three words. Let the board grow, week by week shows how to bring them back.
  3. Swap a word that never came up for one that fits your routines better.
  4. If the buttons were hard to see or to hit, choose fewer columns under Caretaker Settings > Board Display > Grid Size, or try the Menu layout.
  5. Add a second routine if the first one is going well, or replace one that never happened.
  6. If nothing seems to have happened yet, that is common at this stage. Change one thing at a time: a more motivating word or photo, a recorded familiar voice, or more modelling in the routine the person enjoys most.
  7. Tell everyone the new words, and update the note on the fridge.

Two weeks is a first look back, not a verdict. If something worries you, talk to a speech and language therapist.
