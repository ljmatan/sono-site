Starting school with AAC: a handover that actually helps

A one-page profile, a short demonstration and a few agreed words can make the difference between a device that is used at school and one that stays in the bag.

Not medical advice. This article shares general information and everyday experience. It is not medical, therapeutic or diagnostic advice. For decisions about your child, talk to their doctor, speech and language therapist or another qualified professional.

A new school year brings a new room, a new teacher and often a new teaching assistant. Everyone is busy. In that bustle, a communication device can quietly end up in a bag, brought out only at designated times. The fix is rarely a longer document. It is a short, practical handover that shows people what to do in the first week.

Start with one page, not a folder

Teachers do not need the whole history of the app. They need to know how to include this pupil today. A single page works well:

  • How the pupil communicates now: speech, gestures, eye pointing, signs, a paper board, the app, or a mix. Say what to look for.
  • The device is their voice: it stays within reach during lessons, lunch and play, not locked in a cupboard.
  • Three to five words we are modelling this month: for example MORE, STOP, HELP, LOOK, FINISHED. Show where they are on the board.
  • How long to wait: a short prompt such as “count to ten before repeating or rephrasing”.
  • What not to do: no quizzes (“show me the answer on your tablet”), no taking the device away as a sanction, no speaking for the pupil when they are trying.
  • Who to ask: your name and contact details, and the speech and language therapist’s name if they are involved.

Add a photo of the pupil’s board with the key words circled. It is easier to glance at than a paragraph.

Show, don’t just tell

Ten minutes of modelling in the classroom is worth more than a page of instructions. Ask to join a short session in the first week. Use the board in front of the teacher:

“Let’s find our coats. COAT… and then OUTSIDE.”

Then wait. Show how a tap produces a voice, how the sentence strip works, and how the pupil can say STOP or HELP. Invite the teacher to try one word. The goal is not to make them an expert; it is to make the device feel normal.

Agree on the small things

A few practical agreements prevent most problems:

  • Where the device lives during the day, and who charges it.
  • What happens at break and lunch: who carries it, where it stays, how it is protected.
  • How the pupil asks to use the toilet, or says they are unwell, without having to fight for the device.
  • What to do when the device is unavailable: a printed board, a paper version of the key words, or another agreed method.
  • How the school will tell you about wins and difficulties, without waiting for a formal review.

If the school has its own AAC system, agree which one is used for what. Two systems can coexist, but everyone needs to know which is the pupil’s main voice.

Prepare the other children

Children are usually curious, not unkind. A short, matter-of-fact explanation helps: “This is how Alex talks. You can talk to Alex normally. If you don’t understand, wait, or ask Alex to show you again.”

Avoid making one child the official interpreter. Friends can help, but the pupil’s own communication should stay central. A whole-class lesson about communication differences can be useful, but it should not turn the pupil into a demonstration.

The first review

After two or three weeks, ask three questions:

  1. Which words are being used naturally, and which are still only used in planned sessions?
  2. When does the device stay in the bag, and why?
  3. What would make the biggest difference next week?

Adjust one thing at a time. The Working together guide has more on sharing the plan across home and school. A new routine, a new word set and a new seating plan all at once is too much for everyone.

A note on rights and safety

The Communication Bill of Rights is clear that people have the right to a working communication system at all times. That includes school. A device is not a reward for good behaviour and should not be removed as a punishment. If the school’s behaviour policy makes this difficult, raise it early with the headteacher and the speech and language therapist.

Starting school is a big transition for the whole family. The aim is not a perfect handover. It is a school where the pupil’s voice is expected, noticed and answered.
