Birthdays and celebrations: AAC in the loud, happy moments

Parties are busy, noisy and full of relatives. A little planning helps the person using AAC join in, be understood and step out when it gets too much.

Questo articolo non è ancora stato tradotto nella tua lingua, quindi è mostrato in inglese. Aiutaci a tradurlo

Non è un consiglio medico. Questo articolo condivide informazioni generali ed esperienze quotidiane. Non è un consiglio medico, terapeutico o diagnostico. Per le decisioni che riguardano tuo figlio o tua figlia, parlane con il medico, il logopedista o un altro professionista qualificato.

Birthdays, weddings, holidays and family gatherings are supposed to be happy. They are also loud, crowded, unpredictable and full of people who want to talk. For someone who uses AAC, a celebration can be one of the hardest communication environments of the year.

The goal is not a perfect party. It is a celebration where the person is present, has a voice and can leave the room without a meltdown.

Prepare the words before the day

A few symbols added for the occasion can make a big difference:

  • Social: HELLO, BYE, THANK YOU, PLEASE, HAPPY BIRTHDAY, CONGRATULATIONS.
  • Party things: CAKE, PRESENT, BALLOON, MUSIC, SING.
  • Needs: MORE, ALL DONE, DRINK, TOILET, HELP, QUIET, OUTSIDE.
  • Feelings: HAPPY, EXCITED, TOO LOUD, TIRED, OVERWHELMED.

Keep them close to the main page or in a temporary category. If the board is rebuilt for the party, keep the core words in their usual places.

Give relatives a job, not a lecture

Most relatives want to connect but do not know how. A short, specific role works better than a general instruction to “be supportive”:

  • “Ask Alex a yes/no question and then wait.”
  • “If you don’t understand, ask Alex to show you again.”
  • “The board stays on the table; it’s Alex’s voice.”
  • “Please don’t say ‘show me’ or take the tablet to demonstrate.”

If a relative asks a blunt question (“Will they ever talk?”), a calm, short answer is enough: “We don’t know. Right now this gives Alex a way to communicate.” You do not owe anyone a full explanation at a party. The Explaining AAC to grandparents and relatives post has a longer script.

Protect a quiet corner

Every celebration needs a retreat. Agree on a quiet room, a hallway or a seat outside where the person can go without having to explain. Put a symbol for QUIET or OUTSIDE on the board. Let them use it without being quizzed.

If the person is a child, an adult may need to help them leave. If the person is an adult, respect their choice to step out and come back.

Keep the device safe and available

Parties are messy. Cake, drinks, candles and small children are all hazards. A wipe-clean case, a paper backup and a charged battery help. Do not put the device on a high shelf “so it doesn’t get broken”. A voice that is out of reach is not available.

If the person does not want to use the device at the party, that is their choice. Keep it nearby anyway. They may change their mind.

Model the social words

Celebrations are full of scripted language, which makes them good places to model:

“HAPPY BIRTHDAY!”

“THANK YOU for the present.”

“MORE cake?”

“It’s TOO LOUD. Let’s go outside.”

Model without making the person perform. If they tap or sign, respond. If they do not, keep the moment going.

Opening presents

Presents can be exciting and overwhelming. Give the person time to open at their own pace. Model OPEN, HELP, WOW, LOOK and THANK YOU. If they want to stop, let them stop and come back later.

Do not make a present a test: “Say thank you before you open it.” A prompt in front of an audience can feel humiliating. Model the word and let the person choose whether to use it.

Plan the exit

The end of a celebration is a transition like any other. Give a warning, show ALL DONE or BYE, and leave before everyone is exhausted. A shorter visit that ends well is better than a long one that ends in tears.

A small checklist

  • Add a few party words to the board.
  • Charge the device and pack a paper backup.
  • Agree on a quiet space.
  • Give one relative one specific job.
  • Plan the exit before you arrive.

The person does not have to be the life of the party. They just need to be there as themselves, with a way to say what they need.
