Choosing the first words

Which words should go on the board first, and why? Here is what guidance and research say, and where to find those words in Sono; every person is different, so choose them together with the person's speech and language therapist where you can.

On this page

  1. Core words and personal words
  2. The right to say no
  3. Feelings, pain and asking for help
  4. Let the board grow, week by week
  5. Where the first words are

Core words and personal words # — Copy link to this section

Most of what anyone says is built from a small set of words. ASHA, the American Speech-Language-Hearing Association, calls them core words: high-frequency words that make up about 80% of the words most people use every day. They are small words like MORE, STOP, HELP, GO, WANT, ALL DONE, LIKE and NOT, and each one works in many situations: MORE juice, MORE tickles, MORE music.

Personal words are the other kind: the person's own people, foods, toys and places. Professionals call them fringe words. They come up less often, but they name the people and things in the person's own life, and they are different for every person. ASHA notes that combining core and personal words can make a communication system get used more often.

You don't have to choose between them. A review of core word lists for young children found that such lists may leave out many of the kinds of words that make up children's very first words, so on their own they may not be the best guide for choosing a young beginner's words.

How to do it in Sono

  1. Start with two or three core words. MORE, STOP, HELP, NOT and ALL DONE are in Basics; GO, WANT and LIKE are in Actions.
  2. Look for personal words on the built-in board first: JUICE, BALL, BUBBLES, MOM and DAD are all there.
  3. Add what's missing as photos in Caretaker Settings > My Symbols: their own cup, the family dog, grandma, a favourite snack.
  4. Use both kinds together when you model. For example:
    • You say: "More juice?" You tap: MORE, then JUICE.
    • You say: "Let's go and see grandma!" You tap: GO, then your photo of grandma.
    • You say: "You like the ball!" You tap: LIKE, then BALL.

Sources

  • ASHA — Augmentative and Alternative Communication (Practice Portal)
  • AssistiveWare — 4 things every AAC system needs
  • Laubscher & Light (2020) — Core vocabulary lists for young children and considerations for early language development: a narrative review. Augmentative and Alternative Communication 36(1)

The right to say no # — Copy link to this section

Being able to refuse is a right, not bad behaviour. The Communication Bill of Rights, drawn up by a joint committee of US professional associations, includes the right to ask for or refuse objects, actions, events and people. ASHA, the American Speech-Language-Hearing Association, lists rejecting and protesting among the things core words let a person do. And Carole Zangari, a speech and language therapy professor who writes for families and professionals, says learners need the words to tell us when they don't like something. Her advice, in short: model saying no yourself, put words to the person's own refusals, honour them whenever possible, especially early on, and practise first in calm, happy moments.

How to do it in Sono

  1. Keep NO (in Manners) and STOP, NOT and ALL DONE (in Basics) on the board from day one, even when they aren't this week's words. In the Advanced and Menu layouts, No and Stop are also on the Quick button.
  2. Model your own refusals. For example:
    • You say: "Not the red one. The blue one!" You tap: NOT.
    • You say: "No more coffee for me, I'm all done." You tap: NO, then ALL DONE.
  3. Put words to theirs. If they push the plate away, say "You're all done!" and tap ALL DONE.
  4. When the person taps NO, STOP or ALL DONE, act on it whenever it's safe: stop the tickling, take the plate away.
  5. When you can't, say so and explain: "You said stop. We need to finish brushing your teeth, then we're done."
  6. Keep a way to say yes and no without the device, too. Project Core, a programme of the University of North Carolina, recommends that every learner has a natural way to show yes and no, such as nodding or blinking, because a device isn't always at hand. Ask their speech and language therapist which way suits them.

Sources

  • NJC / ASHA — Communication Bill of Rights, 3rd edition
  • ASHA — Augmentative and Alternative Communication (Practice Portal)
  • PrAACtical AAC (Zangari, 2020) — Learning to protest: 5 considerations for AAC intervention
  • Project Core (Center for Literacy and Disability Studies, University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill) — Frequently asked questions

Feelings, pain and asking for help # — Copy link to this section

Some words matter most on the day they're needed, and that day is a hard time to learn them. A study of pain-related words starts from a simple point: children with significant communication difficulties who are in pain need a way to say so to get the right care. In that study, adults who use augmentative and alternative communication themselves reviewed the word list and stressed that the words should be chosen for each person. The Communication Bill of Rights also includes the right to express feelings.

How to do it in Sono

  1. Keep HELP (in Basics) and HURT (in Feelings) on the board from the start. In the Advanced and Menu layouts, Help is also on the Quick button.
  2. Feelings has two parts: Emotions, with HAPPY, SAD, ANGRY, SCARED and more, and Body, with HURT, SORE, SICK, TIRED, HUNGRY, THIRSTY and more. In the Advanced and Menu layouts, a chip for each narrows the category down.
  3. On the Start tab (Advanced and Simple layouts), the sentence starter "It hurts…" is spoken with one tap.
  4. Model feelings as they happen, yours and theirs, on good days as well as hard ones. For example:
    • You say: "Ouch, my knee hurts!" You tap: HURT.
    • You say: "You're laughing. You're happy!" You tap: HAPPY.
    • You say: "This jar is stuck. I need help." You tap: HELP.
  5. When the person taps HURT, SICK or HELP, respond as you would if they had said it out loud.
  6. Sono has no symbols for parts of the body. The person may point to show where it hurts; if they need words for that too, ask their speech and language therapist which ones, and add them as photos in My Symbols.

Sono is not meant for emergencies. Make sure everyone around the person knows the other ways they show pain or distress (Is Sono a therapy?).

Sources

  • Johnson, Bornman & Tönsing (2016) — An exploration of pain-related vocabulary: implications for AAC use with children. Augmentative and Alternative Communication 32(4)
  • NJC / ASHA — Communication Bill of Rights, 3rd edition

Let the board grow, week by week # — Copy link to this section

A small board helps at the start, but a board that stays small limits what a person can say. Project Core, a programme of the University of North Carolina, recommends that every learner has access to at least 36 core words; some start with fewer on a page and work up to the whole set. AssistiveWare, a company that makes communication apps, describes the goal as a vocabulary with many words of every kind, so that a person can do more than ask for things by name, and its guide to core words recommends adding new words regularly and combining them with the words from earlier weeks. ASHA, the American Speech-Language-Hearing Association, adds that everyone's vocabulary changes over time.

How to do it in Sono

  1. Every week or two, add one to three words: the next small word, and a personal word when something new comes into the person's life.
  2. Bring words back with Restore under Caretaker Settings > Hidden Symbols, or switch a category on again under Caretaker Settings > Categories.
  3. Don't hide words the person already uses, even if you aren't modelling them this week.
  4. ASHA notes that words often keep the same place on a board, so finding them takes less memory and movement planning. In Sono, a word you bring back returns to its old place and moves the symbols after it along, so show the person where things are now, and keep the same Grid Size for a while.
  5. Model a little beyond what the person uses. AssistiveWare suggests modelling at least one word more than the person currently uses: if they tap single words, model two-word phrases.
  6. Combine new words with familiar ones. For example:
    • You say: "You don't like it? Okay." You tap: NOT, then LIKE.
    • You say: "Let's play again!" You tap: PLAY, then AGAIN.

Show everyone who models where the new words are.

Sources

  • Project Core (Center for Literacy and Disability Studies, University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill) — Frequently asked questions
  • AssistiveWare — 4 things every AAC system needs
  • AssistiveWare — Core word teaching strategies
  • ASHA — Augmentative and Alternative Communication (Practice Portal)
  • AssistiveWare — Modeling: use AAC to teach AAC

Where the first words are # — Copy link to this section

A quick map of the words in this section. Each line names the category, then the chip inside it. In the Advanced and Menu layouts, chips under the category narrow it down; in the Simple layout, the whole category shows at once. In Advanced and Menu, Search on the sentence strip finds any symbol that isn't hidden.

Small words that work everywhere

  • Basics, Core: HELP, STOP, MORE, NOT, ALL DONE, READY, MINE
  • Actions, Mind: WANT, LIKE, NEED, FEEL
  • Actions, Movement: GO, PLAY, OPEN
  • Actions, Routine: EAT, DRINK, WAIT
  • Time, How Often: AGAIN

Yes and no

  • Manners, Responses: YES, NO, NO THANKS, OKAY, MAYBE

Feelings and the body

  • Feelings, Emotions: HAPPY, SAD, ANGRY, SCARED
  • Feelings, Body: HURT, SORE, SICK, TIRED, HUNGRY, THIRSTY

Personal words

  • People, Family: MOM, DAD, GRANDMA, GRANDPA
  • Food, Drinks: WATER, JUICE, MILK
  • Things, Play & Tech: BALL, BUBBLES
  • Your own photos: the My Symbols tab, or the My Symbols tile in the Menu layout.

One tap away

  • Quick: in the Advanced and Menu layouts, the lightning bolt on the sentence strip opens ready phrases such as Help, Stop, More, No and Wait, spoken at once. Choose them under Caretaker Settings > Quick Phrases.
  • Start: in the Advanced and Simple layouts, sentence starters such as "I want…", "I like…" and "It hurts…" say the start of a sentence with one tap. Choose them under Caretaker Settings > Sentence Starters.
