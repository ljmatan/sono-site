Calm-down plans: AAC for the moments after a meltdown

When someone is overwhelmed, more words usually make things harder. How to plan a calm-down routine, keep communication available and repair afterwards.

Not medical advice. This article shares general information and everyday experience. It is not medical, therapeutic or diagnostic advice. For decisions about your child, talk to their doctor, speech and language therapist or another qualified professional.

A meltdown is not a performance. It usually means someone is overwhelmed by noise, change, pain, fatigue or demands. In that moment, asking a person to find the right symbol can add another demand to someone who is already overloaded.

The Calm ways through hard moments guide covers the wider plan; this post focuses on what to do in the moment and afterwards.

A calm-down plan is not about teaching a new word during a crisis. It is about preparing a routine, a space and a few familiar signals before the crisis happens.

Plan in a calm moment

When everyone is regulated, talk about what helps. If the person can take part, ask. If not, observe and ask the people who know them.

Write a short plan:

  • Early signs: what does the person do before things escalate? Pace, stim, go quiet, repeat a phrase, cover their ears.
  • What helps: quiet, movement, a weighted blanket, a favourite song, dim lights, a walk, being alone.
  • What does not help: too many questions, being held, eye contact, a crowded room, a device thrust into their hands.
  • Words to keep available: STOP, HELP, BREAK, QUIET, ALL DONE, HURT.
  • Who to call: one or two people who can support without taking over.

Keep the plan short enough to remember. A long document is not useful in a crisis.

Reduce language, not presence

During a meltdown, use fewer words and a calm voice. Offer one choice at a time, or none at all. Stay nearby if that helps; give space if that helps.

You can still model one familiar symbol, but do not demand a response. A tap on STOP or a look toward the door may be the only communication available.

Do not say “use your words” or “show me what you want”. The person may not have access to language in that moment. Behaviour is communication too.

Keep the device safe and available

A device can be thrown, dropped or used to hit during a meltdown. Safety comes first. If the device is at risk, move it to a safe place within sight, and use a paper backup or a simple gesture.

Do not remove the device permanently as a punishment. Once the person is calm, bring it back. The device is still their voice.

Create a calm-down space

A predictable space helps: a corner with cushions, a tent, a quiet room, a familiar chair. It should not feel like a punishment. Call it “the quiet place” or whatever the person understands.

Put a small communication board in the space. Include STOP, HELP, MORE, ALL DONE, QUIET and the name of a trusted person. Keep it simple.

Repair afterwards

When the person is calm, reconnect. Do not start with a lecture or a demand to apologise. Try:

“That was hard. You were very upset. I’m here.”

“You said STOP. I stopped. Good.”

“Next time, do you want the quiet room or a walk?”

Model the words on the board. If the person wants to talk about it, listen. If not, let it go and try again later.

Look for patterns

After a few incidents, look for triggers: noise, hunger, pain, transitions, a particular person, a busy day. A simple note can help. If the pattern suggests a medical or sensory issue, talk to a doctor, occupational therapist or speech and language therapist.

A final note

A calm-down plan is not a behaviour modification programme. It is a way to reduce demands, protect safety and keep communication available when language is hardest. The person is not giving you a hard time. They are having a hard time.
