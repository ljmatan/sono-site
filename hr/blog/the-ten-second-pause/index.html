The ten-second pause: the communication skill most of us skip

Waiting feels awkward, but a quiet pause often does more for communication than another question. What to try, and how it fits with modelling.

Ovaj članak još nije preveden na tvoj jezik pa je prikazan na engleskom. Pomozi nam ga prevesti

Nije medicinski savjet. Ovaj članak donosi opće informacije i iskustva iz svakodnevice. Nije medicinski, terapijski ni dijagnostički savjet. O odlukama koje se tiču vašeg djeteta razgovarajte s liječnikom, logopedom ili drugim kvalificiranim stručnjakom.

You ask a question. Two seconds pass. The silence feels enormous, so you ask again, rephrase it, or answer for the person. It is a very human reaction. It is also one of the easiest ways to accidentally close a conversation.

Communication partners do a lot of quiet work. Research on partner training keeps returning to the same ingredients: modelling, waiting expectantly, and responding to whatever the person does. The pause is the part most of us skip.

Why waiting is hard

Most conversation moves quickly. A gap of three or four seconds feels like a breakdown, especially when you are worried that the person is not understanding you. So we fill the gap with more words. For someone who needs extra time to process language, find a symbol, or plan a movement, that extra speech can bury the original message.

Waiting is not the same as doing nothing. It is an active message: I have finished my turn. I am listening. Take the time you need.

What ten seconds looks like

Try this once, in a calm moment:

  1. Say one short sentence, such as “The bubbles are ready.”
  2. Model one word on the board: BUBBLES, or MORE if that is the word you are working on.
  3. Look at the person expectantly. Keep your face relaxed.
  4. Count ten seconds silently. Resist the urge to repeat the question.
  5. Notice anything: a glance at the board, a reach, a sound, a change in breathing, a tap. Any of these is a response.

Ten seconds can feel strange at first. That is normal. You are not testing the person; you are giving them the same turn length you would give a slow-speaking conversation partner.

If nothing happens

Nothing happening is information, not failure. Try one of these, then wait again:

  • Simplify: one word instead of a sentence.
  • Offer a choice: “JUICE or WATER?” Show both symbols or both objects.
  • Model again: tap the word yourself, slowly, then pause.
  • Change the activity: a tired person at the end of a long day may have nothing left for a language task.
  • Accept another route: a point, a gesture or a look is communication too.

If weeks of daily modelling and waiting bring no reaction at all, that is a good thing to discuss with a speech and language therapist. It does not mean you did anything wrong.

Four habits that cancel the pause

  • Firing questions. “Do you want juice? Juice? Can you show me juice?” gives the person several things to answer at once.
  • “Say it on the tablet.” This turns communication into a test. Model the word and move on.
  • Answering for them. If you already know, it is still worth leaving a small gap for the person to join in.
  • Only responding to the device. A look or a reach can be the beginning of a message. Respond to the attempt, then model the symbol that fits.

In Sono

Keep the board open and within reach before you pause. The How to gain interest guide explains modelling and waiting step by step. If you want to see which words the person is drawn to, the Recents tab and Message History can show patterns over time without turning the day into a data-collection exercise.

The pause costs nothing and needs no special equipment. It is often the first thing to try when communication feels stuck.
