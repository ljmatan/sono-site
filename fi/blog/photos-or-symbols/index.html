Photos or symbols? Choosing what the person actually recognises

A clear photo can work better than a neat icon, or the other way round. How to choose images by recognition rather than tidiness, and keep the board consistent.

Tätä artikkelia ei ole vielä käännetty kielellesi, joten se näytetään englanniksi. Auta meitä kääntämään se

Ei lääketieteellinen neuvo. Tämä artikkeli jakaa yleistä tietoa ja arjen kokemuksia. Se ei ole lääketieteellinen, terapeuttinen eikä diagnostinen neuvo. Keskustele lastasi koskevista päätöksistä hänen lääkärinsä, puheterapeuttinsa tai muun pätevän ammattilaisen kanssa.

A communication board can look beautifully organised and still fail, because the person cannot tell what a symbol means. The question is not which image is prettier. It is which image the person recognises quickly, in the middle of a real moment.

Photos and symbol sets each have strengths. Most boards end up using both.

When a photo helps

Photos are often easier to recognise when the word is:

  • A specific person. A photo of grandma is clearer than a generic drawing of an older woman.
  • A specific place. The actual front door, school gate or favourite cafe.
  • A personal object. The person’s own cup, blanket, wheelchair or toy.
  • A food or package. The exact biscuit packet may be more meaningful than a generic biscuit icon.
  • A pet. A photo of the family dog beats a cartoon dog.

Photos can also carry emotional meaning. A picture of a much-loved grandparent may motivate communication more than a neat symbol.

When a symbol helps

Symbols are often better when the word is:

  • Abstract. Words such as MORE, STOP, WAIT, HELP, LIKE and FINISHED are hard to photograph.
  • A category. “Food” or “drink” is easier to show with a symbol than with one specific meal.
  • Used across settings. A clear symbol transfers from home to school more easily than a photo of your kitchen.
  • Likely to change. Children grow, haircuts change, places close. A symbol stays stable.

Symbols also keep a board visually calm. Too many photos can make a page busy and slow to scan.

Match the image to the word

A useful rule is: photo for the particular, symbol for the general.

  • Photo for Grandma, the family car, the school gate, the dog.
  • Symbol for MORE, STOP, GO, HELP, WANT, ALL DONE.
  • Photo or symbol for a favourite food, depending on what the person recognises.

Consistency matters more than style. If STOP is a red octagon in one place, do not use a red circle somewhere else for the same word. If a photo is used for “cup”, use the same photo every time until the word is established.

Test recognition without a quiz

You do not need to ask “show me the cup” ten times a day. Watch instead:

  • Which symbol does the person reach for without help?
  • Which one do they look at when you model it?
  • Which one causes a pause or a frown?
  • Which photo do they pick up or point to in a book?

If a symbol is consistently ignored, it may be the image rather than the word. Try a photo, a different symbol set, or a larger button.

Avoid confusing images

Some symbols are too similar: a cup and a glass, a spoon and a fork, “open” and “close”. Check the board from the person’s viewing distance, not from close up. If two buttons look alike at arm’s length, change one.

Also avoid:

  • photos with distracting backgrounds;
  • pictures that show several objects at once;
  • outdated photos of people or places;
  • images that are culturally confusing or infantilising;
  • the same photo used for two different words.

Keep privacy in mind

Photos on a communication board can include family members, medical equipment or private places. Ask before adding a photo of someone else, especially on a device that leaves the house. For adults, the person should decide what images are on their own board.

Build the mix slowly

Start with the words the person uses most. Add one photo or symbol at a time and model it in context. A board that changes every week is hard to learn, even if every individual image is clear.
