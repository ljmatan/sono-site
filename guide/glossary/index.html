Usage guide

Words professionals use

The terms therapists and schools use, explained in plain language, each with where it shows up in Sono. Words differ a little between countries, so ask whenever one is new to you.

On this page

  1. Communication, partners and modelling
  2. Kinds of communication aids
  3. Core and fringe words, colours and motor planning
  4. The professionals you'll meet

Communication, partners and modelling # — Copy link to this section

Augmentative and alternative communication

  • What it means: any way of communicating that adds to speech (augmentative) or replaces it (alternative). You will often see it shortened to AAC. The Royal College of Speech and Language Therapists, the UK professional body, divides it into unaided ways, which use only the body, such as signing and facial expressions, and aided ways, which use tools, such as communication boards, books or apps. Using several side by side is what Speech Pathology Australia, the professional association for speech pathologists in Australia, calls multimodal communication.
  • In Sono: Sono is an aided way. The person's gestures, sounds and looks still count alongside it.

Communication partner

  • What it means: anyone who talks with the person, such as family, teachers and friends. AssistiveWare, the makers of another communication app, lists helpful skills, among them modelling, commenting instead of quizzing, pausing and waiting, answering every attempt and accepting every way the person communicates.
  • In Sono: see Give time, then answer every attempt and Everyone around the person.

Modelling (aided language input)

  • What it means: using the person's board yourself while you talk, so they see how it is used. AssistiveWare lists other names for it, such as aided language stimulation, and stresses that the person doesn't have to copy you.
  • In Sono: tap one or two key words as you say them, as Model: use Sono yourself while you talk shows.

Presume competence

  • What it means: assuming that the person can learn, think and understand, and has something to say, even before you can be sure. AssistiveWare calls this the least dangerous assumption: not believing in someone and doing nothing is far riskier than believing in them and starting. ASHA, the American Speech-Language-Hearing Association, likewise says there are no prerequisites.
  • In Sono: talk to the person as someone who understands, and keep words for every purpose within reach: feelings, comments, NO and STOP as well as requests.

Kinds of communication aids # — Copy link to this section

Low-tech and high-tech

  • What it means: low-tech aids have no battery: picture boards, communication books, symbol cards. High-tech aids are powered: tablets, phones and devices that speak. The Royal College of Speech and Language Therapists, the UK professional body, calls these old terms: most people in the UK now say paper-based and powered, and powered is not always better than paper.
  • In Sono: Sono is high-tech. Caretaker Settings > Print Boards makes a paper copy: a PDF of one category or of all of them. Tap Share PDF to save or send it, then print it.

Speech-generating device

  • What it means: an electronic aid that speaks out loud, with a synthetic or recorded voice. Communication Matters, a UK charity, calls them voice output communication aids, from a single button with one recorded message to screens of symbols, phrases or letters. Speech Pathology Australia, the professional association for speech pathologists in Australia, separates devices built for people who communicate this way from mainstream devices: ordinary tablets and phones with a communication app.
  • In Sono: Sono is an app on a mainstream phone or tablet. It speaks with a synthetic voice on the device, or with a recording you make for a symbol in Caretaker Settings > Customise Symbols. If a dedicated device is recommended, ask how it and Sono would fit together.

Picture Exchange Communication System

  • What it means: a structured programme, usually called PECS, developed in the US in 1985 by Andy Bondy and Lori Frost. The person hands a picture to a partner to ask for something, and the partner responds straight away. Pyramid Educational Consultants, who teach it, describe six phases, from swapping single pictures to building sentences on a strip and commenting, and recommend their training.
  • In Sono: nothing is handed over; the person taps and the app speaks. If the person already uses PECS, ask the people who set it up before adding Sono.

Sources

  • RCSLT — Augmentative and alternative communication (AAC)
  • Communication Matters — About AAC systems
  • Speech Pathology Australia — Augmentative and alternative communication: fact sheet (PDF)
  • Pyramid Educational Consultants — Picture Exchange Communication System (PECS)

Core and fringe words, colours and motor planning # — Copy link to this section

Core and fringe words

  • What it means: core words are short words that fit almost any situation, such as more, stop, go, want, not and what. ASHA, the American Speech-Language-Hearing Association, describes them as the high-frequency words that make up about 80% of the words most people use every day. Fringe words are very specific, mostly names of particular things: banana, giraffe, grandma.
  • In Sono: Basics holds many core words (MORE, STOP, HELP, NOT, ALL DONE, WHAT) and Actions more (GO, WANT, LIKE). Fringe words fill Food, Animals, Places and Things; the most personal ones belong in My Symbols.

Fitzgerald Key colours

  • What it means: colouring symbols by the job each word does in a sentence. Edith Fitzgerald, a deaf teacher, created the original key to teach grammar to deaf students. One practitioner website lists the modified version as yellow for pronouns, green for actions, orange for things, blue for describing words, purple for questions, pink for social words, and red for no, not and urgent words. It says the most important thing is to keep the colours consistent.
  • In Sono: built-in symbols carry their category's colour. A symbol you make in My Symbols takes the colour of the Word Type you pick: People yellow, Actions green, Things orange, Feelings blue, Questions purple, Basics pink, Important red. See Colour coding.

Motor planning

  • What it means: the brain planning a movement. A movement repeated often becomes automatic, like entering a code you know by heart, so a word that always stays in the same place takes less effort to find. AssistiveWare, the makers of another communication app, explains that keeping often-used words in the same places makes use of motor planning.
  • In Sono: categories and the symbols in them keep a fixed order, but a new Grid Size or layout moves symbols, and hiding or showing a symbol shifts the ones after it. See When to change words or layout.

The professionals you'll meet # — Copy link to this section

Speech and language therapist

  • What it means: the professional who assesses communication and, when speech isn't enough, helps find the right communication system and learn to use it. The title depends on the country: speech and language therapist in the UK, speech-language pathologist in the US and Canada, speech pathologist in Australia. You may see it shortened to SLT or SLP. The Royal College of Speech and Language Therapists, the UK professional body, says they often take the lead in introducing augmentative and alternative communication, as part of a team with the person, their family, teachers and occupational therapists. Speech Pathology Australia, the professional association for speech pathologists in Australia, adds that they teach family and friends ways to help.
  • In Sono: they can help pick the first words, the layout and the Grid Size, and read your weekly notes with you. See Review goals with a professional.

Occupational therapist

  • What it means: often shortened to OT; in this field, the therapist who helps with the physical side of using a communication aid. The same UK body says speech and language therapists work with occupational therapists to find a way of using the aid that suits each person's physical abilities. ASHA, the American Speech-Language-Hearing Association, says occupational and physical therapists help with positioning, such as how the person sits and where the device goes, and with how the person selects, for example by reaching and tapping.
  • In Sono: settings worth trying together are Grid Size (fewer columns for bigger buttons), the Menu layout, Circle tiles and Tap Behavior. Sono is used by touch, a stylus or a keyboard. It has no built-in switch scanning (choosing with one or two large buttons instead of touching the screen) or eye-gaze control, so if touching the screen is too hard, ask them about other ways to use a communication aid. See Tapping and coordination.
