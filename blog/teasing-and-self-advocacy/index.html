Teasing, bullying and self-advocacy: words that help at school

A communication device can attract unwanted attention. How to prepare clear words, involve the school and support a child or teenager to speak up for themselves.

Not medical advice. This article shares general information and everyday experience. It is not medical, therapeutic or diagnostic advice. For decisions about your child, talk to their doctor, speech and language therapist or another qualified professional.

A communication device can make a child stand out. Most classmates adapt quickly, but some tease, imitate the voice, grab the device or exclude the child from games. It can be painful, and it can make a person stop using their voice in public.

This post is general information, not a legal or safeguarding guide. If a child is being bullied, involve the school and follow their safeguarding process.

Name it clearly

Teasing becomes bullying when it is repeated and intended to hurt or control. It can be:

  • imitating the device’s voice to mock the person;
  • taking or hiding the device;
  • blocking access to friends or games;
  • spreading photos or videos without consent;
  • saying the person is “stupid” because they use AAC;
  • excluding them from group work or play.

Do not dismiss it as “kids being kids”. Repeated humiliation has an impact, and a communication device should never be the price of belonging.

Prepare self-advocacy words

A person who uses AAC needs ways to say:

  • STOP — an immediate boundary.
  • NO — refusal.
  • DON’T — objection.
  • HELP — get support.
  • TELL — report what happened.
  • SAFE / NOT SAFE — describe a situation.
  • MY TURN / WAIT — manage a group.
  • I DON’T LIKE THAT — a clear statement.

Put these where they are quick to reach. If they are buried in a subcategory, the person may not find them in the moment. Quick Phrases can hold a few full sentences if the app supports them.

Practise in calm moments

Role-play can help, but keep it low-pressure. Try a simple script:

“If someone takes your device, you can say STOP and then TELL a teacher.”

Model the words, then let the person try. Do not turn it into a test. Practise with a trusted adult first, then with a friend.

Involve the school early

Do not wait for an incident to become a pattern. Ask for a short meeting with the teacher, teaching assistant and SENCO or inclusion lead. Bring:

  • a one-page profile of how the person communicates;
  • examples of what has happened, with dates if possible;
  • a clear request: “The device stays with Alex at all times. Please teach the class how to respond.”
  • a plan for what to do if the device is taken or damaged.

Schools should have a behaviour and anti-bullying policy. Ask how it applies to disability-based bullying. If the school is dismissive, escalate to the headteacher or the local authority, and involve the speech and language therapist if possible. The Starting school with AAC post includes a one-page handover that can be adapted for this conversation.

Teach the class, not just the target

A short, matter-of-fact lesson about communication differences can reduce teasing. It should not single out the child or turn them into a demonstration. Topics could include:

  • what AAC is;
  • how the device speaks;
  • how to wait and listen;
  • what to do if you do not understand;
  • why taking the device is not funny.

If the child wants to help explain, let them. If not, the adult can do it without using the child as an example.

Protect the device

Practical steps:

  • a case and strap;
  • a caretaker lock for settings;
  • a rule that the device stays with the person;
  • a named place for it at break and lunch;
  • a paper backup if it is taken or broken.

Do not tell the child to leave the device in their bag to avoid attention. That solves the bullying by removing the voice.

Support the person’s feelings

Being teased is not the person’s fault. They may feel embarrassed, angry or afraid. Listen without minimising. Do not say “just ignore it” if they cannot.

If the person is not ready to report it, help them find one trusted adult and one phrase they can use. Self-advocacy is a skill, and it grows with support.

A small plan

This week, add STOP, HELP and TELL to the main board. Show them to the person. Tell one adult at school that these words exist and what they mean. Ask the school to review its anti-bullying plan for disability-based incidents.

A voice should not have to be defended every day. But until the environment changes, clear words and allies make a difference.
