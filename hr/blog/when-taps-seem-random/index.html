When every tap seems random: respond first, correct later

Exploring a symbol board can look like chaos. What the tapping might mean, and how to respond without turning communication into a test.

Ovaj članak još nije preveden na tvoj jezik pa je prikazan na engleskom. Pomozi nam ga prevesti

Nije medicinski savjet. Ovaj članak donosi opće informacije i iskustva iz svakodnevice. Nije medicinski, terapijski ni dijagnostički savjet. O odlukama koje se tiču vašeg djeteta razgovarajte s liječnikom, logopedom ili drugim kvalificiranim stručnjakom.

A child runs a hand across the tablet and a voice says “banana, stop, blue, grandma”. They tap the same symbol twenty times. They find the volume button and make the board very loud. It can look as if nothing is being communicated.

Exploration is part of learning. Babies babble before they talk; children learning to use symbols often explore in the same way. The taps are not necessarily a message yet, but they are a way of finding out what the board does and what the people around them do in response.

The first job is cause and effect

Before a symbol can mean “banana”, the person needs to learn that tapping it makes something happen. That is a real step, not a failure. The Exploring by tapping guide covers this stage. Your response is the lesson:

  • They tap STOP. You stop the tickle and say, “Stop? Okay, I’m stopping.”
  • They tap MORE. You give one more push on the swing.
  • They tap a random symbol. You can respond lightly: “Blue! You found blue”, and carry on.

You do not have to obey every random tap as if it were a considered request. But responding to some of them shows that the board has power.

Look for patterns

After a week or two, patterns often appear. One symbol gets tapped at meals. Another gets tapped when the person is upset. A third gets tapped because the sound is funny. Note what you see without turning it into a spreadsheet. The Recents tab and Message History in Sono can help you notice which words come up.

Patterns are more useful than a single correct answer. They tell you what the person is drawn to, which words are worth keeping close, and which parts of the board are confusing.

Respond, then model

When a tap seems meaningful, try this sequence:

  1. Respond to the attempt: “You tapped JUICE.”
  2. Do something if it is safe and possible: pour a little juice, or say “Juice later, after lunch.”
  3. Model the word you think fits: tap JUICE again, or model MORE.
  4. Move on without a quiz.

If the person taps DOG for the cat, you can model CAT once and say, “Cat! Yes, the cat.” There is no need to correct the mistake repeatedly. A cheerful model is enough.

Check the access, not just the intent

Sometimes what looks like random tapping is a motor or visual issue. The button may be too small, the response too slow, or the symbols too similar. Try:

  • fewer columns and larger buttons;
  • a simpler layout;
  • a different symbol style;
  • moving the most important words closer;
  • checking whether the person can see and reach the board comfortably.

An occupational therapist can help if pointing is physically difficult. A speech and language therapist can help if the symbol system itself seems confusing.

Keep the pressure low

The fastest way to make a board feel like a test is to say “show me” or “you know this one”. Exploration is allowed. Mistakes are allowed. The person is not performing for you; they are learning a language.

If the tapping is constant and overwhelming, give the board a rest without removing it. A calm, quiet moment with one or two symbols is often more useful than a busy session with twenty.
