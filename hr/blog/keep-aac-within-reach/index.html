Keep it within reach: access is part of communication

A communication device only works when it is there. Practical ways to keep it charged, close and available without turning it into a reward.

Ovaj članak još nije preveden na tvoj jezik pa je prikazan na engleskom. Pomozi nam ga prevesti

Nije medicinski savjet. Ovaj članak donosi opće informacije i iskustva iz svakodnevice. Nije medicinski, terapijski ni dijagnostički savjet. O odlukama koje se tiču vašeg djeteta razgovarajte s liječnikom, logopedom ili drugim kvalificiranim stručnjakom.

A communication board is not a treat. It is not a therapy session, a reward for good behaviour, or a special activity for the table. It is a voice. And a voice only works when it is within reach.

That sounds obvious, but devices end up in cupboards for all sorts of reasons: fear of damage, screen-time rules, charging routines, behaviour plans, or simply because nobody has agreed where it should live. Each small removal teaches the person that their voice is optional.

The right to a working system

The Communication Bill of Rights, drawn up by a joint committee of US professional associations, includes the right to a working communication system at all times. ISAAC, the International Society for Augmentative and Alternative Communication, makes the same point in its public guidance: a person’s communication system should never be taken away.

This does not mean every device must be in a child’s hands every second. It means the default is availability. Any exception needs a clear, temporary reason and a backup plan.

Make a place for it

A communication tool needs a home in each part of the day:

  • At meals: on the table, within reach, not on a high shelf.
  • In the car: in a bag the person can reach, or on the seat beside them.
  • At school: in the classroom, with an agreed place at break and lunch.
  • In the bathroom: a paper board on the wall or a small waterproof card.
  • At bedtime: somewhere calm and predictable, not locked away.
  • On days out: a charged device plus a paper backup.

If the device is shared between home and school, agree who charges it and when. A flat battery is an access issue, not a behaviour issue. The Caretaker settings guide covers locks, backups and everyday upkeep.

Screen time is different for a voice

Families are often told to limit screens. A screen used as a voice is not the same as a screen used for passive watching. The distinction matters: if the only way a person can ask for help is the device, then turning it off to reduce screen time removes their voice.

That does not mean unlimited entertainment. It means the communication app stays available even when other screen time is finished. If that feels confusing to siblings, explain it simply: “This is how Alex talks.”

Keep a paper backup

Technology fails. Batteries die, apps update, a tablet gets dropped. A printed copy of the key words is not a step backwards; it is a safety net.

Print the main page and the words used most often. Keep a copy in the bag, one at school, and one at home. A paper board also works at the pool, on the beach, in the bath and in places where a device would be damaged.

What not to do

  • Do not take the device away as a punishment for behaviour. If the behaviour is communication, removing the voice makes the problem harder.
  • Do not lock it in a cupboard “for safekeeping” during the day.
  • Do not save it for scheduled practice time. Real communication happens in real moments.
  • Do not make the person ask for the device every time. Their voice should not need permission.

If the person is throwing the device or using it to hurt someone, the answer is a safety plan: a short pause, a paper backup, a soft case, and help from an occupational therapist if needed. It is not a permanent removal.

A small check-in

Once a week, ask: where did the device spend most of its time? If the answer is “in the bag” or “on the shelf”, pick one moment to change. Put it on the table at breakfast. Move the charging cable to the kitchen. Print a backup. Small access changes often produce more communication than a new set of symbols.
