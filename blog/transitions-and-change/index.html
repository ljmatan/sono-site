Transitions: helping change feel manageable with AAC

Stopping a favourite activity or moving to a new place can be the hardest part of the day. Symbols, warnings and choices can make transitions easier.

Not medical advice. This article shares general information and everyday experience. It is not medical, therapeutic or diagnostic advice. For decisions about your child, talk to their doctor, speech and language therapist or another qualified professional.

Most adults can finish a sentence, save a file and then leave for an appointment. A child or adult with a communication difference may not have the words to say “not yet”, “five more minutes” or “I don’t want to stop”. The transition happens anyway, and the distress that follows can look like behaviour rather than communication.

AAC does not remove the difficulty of change. It gives the person a way to see it coming, protest, negotiate and recover. If transitions often end in distress, the Calm ways through hard moments guide has more on reducing demands.

Make the change visible

Spoken explanations disappear quickly. Symbols stay visible. Use them to show what is happening:

  • First–then: “First shopping, then park.” Show both symbols.
  • A countdown: three fingers, a visual timer, or three symbols for the steps.
  • A sequence: shoes, coat, car, school. Point to each as it happens.
  • A change card: a symbol or photo for the new place, shown before leaving.

Keep the board in reach during the transition, not packed in the bag. A child who is being carried to the car may still need to say STOP or HELP.

Give a warning, then follow through

A warning only helps if it is true. “Five minutes” should mean five minutes, not two. If you say “one more push”, do one more push and then stop.

A simple script:

“Two more pushes. Then ALL DONE.”

model MORE and ALL DONE

“One more.”

“ALL DONE. Time to go.”

If the person protests, acknowledge it: “You want MORE. I hear you. We’re all done now. We can come back tomorrow.” Honouring the message does not always mean granting the request. It means not pretending you did not hear it.

Offer real choices inside the change

Transitions are easier when there is some control. Offer choices that fit the situation:

  • “Walk to the car or ride in the buggy?”
  • “Carry the bag or push it?”
  • “Shoes first or coat first?”
  • “One song in the car or one story before we go?”

Avoid offering a choice about whether to leave if leaving is not optional. That creates a negotiation you cannot honour.

Use words that make change easier

Model a small set of transition words:

  • STOP / NO — protest.
  • MORE / AGAIN — request repetition.
  • ALL DONE / FINISHED — closure.
  • WAIT — a pause is coming.
  • GO — moving on.
  • HELP — support with shoes, coat or seatbelt.
  • CHANGE — something different is happening.

Put these where they are easy to reach. If they are buried in a category, the person may not find them in time.

Reduce the language, not the respect

During a transition, many words can make things harder. Use short sentences and one symbol at a time. Give space. Avoid asking several questions in a row.

At the same time, do not talk about the person as if they are not there. “We’re going now” is clearer than a whispered conversation with another adult about how to manage them.

After the hard moment

Once everyone is calm, revisit what happened. Not as a lecture, but as a repair:

“That was hard. You wanted to stay. I said ALL DONE. Next time we can take the timer.”

Model the words again. You are building a shared story of the transition, which makes the next one slightly more predictable.

When transitions are regularly difficult

If every transition leads to distress, look for patterns:

  • Is the person hungry, tired or in pain?
  • Is the warning too short?
  • Is the next activity something they dislike?
  • Is the device available?
  • Is the same adult always delivering the change?

A speech and language therapist or occupational therapist can help if sensory or motor factors are involved. A visual schedule may help more than more words.

A small plan for this week

Pick one transition that is often hard. Add a first–then board, a timer and one choice. Keep the communication board within reach. Do not aim for a perfect transition; aim for one where the person has a way to say what they need.
