When progress feels slow: what to notice besides words

AAC progress is often quiet. Signs to look for, how to keep simple notes, and when to ask for a review instead of blaming yourself.

Tätä artikkelia ei ole vielä käännetty kielellesi, joten se näytetään englanniksi. Auta meitä kääntämään se

Ei lääketieteellinen neuvo. Tämä artikkeli jakaa yleistä tietoa ja arjen kokemuksia. Se ei ole lääketieteellinen, terapeuttinen eikä diagnostinen neuvo. Keskustele lastasi koskevista päätöksistä hänen lääkärinsä, puheterapeuttinsa tai muun pätevän ammattilaisen kanssa.

You have modelled for weeks. You have kept the board on the table, waited, responded, printed a backup. And still, no word. It is easy to conclude that nothing is happening.

Progress in AAC is often quiet. It may show up as a longer look, a hand reaching toward the board, a pause before a protest, or a person who no longer walks away when the device appears. Those are not consolation prizes; they are the early steps of communication.

The timeline is not a straight line

Learning a language takes months and years. Children who speak hear thousands of words a day for more than a year before their first word. A person learning to use symbols is doing something at least as complex, often with motor, sensory or processing differences that make it harder.

Studies of AAC intervention describe gains over time, with variation between people. There is no single week by which a person should produce a word. A lack of independent tapping in week three does not mean the approach has failed.

Signs that something is growing

Look for:

  • Attention: the person looks at the board, or at your hand when you model.
  • Anticipation: they look toward the device before a familiar routine, or reach for it.
  • Participation: they tolerate the board being present for longer, or bring it to you.
  • Intent: a tap, a look or a gesture that seems aimed at something.
  • Reduced frustration: fewer meltdowns around a routine, or a faster recovery.
  • Imitation: they copy a tap, a sound or a gesture, even approximately.
  • Choice: they consistently choose one option over another.

These are worth noting. They are also worth celebrating, even if they do not look like the milestone you expected.

Keep a simple note

A notebook or a note on your phone is enough. Once a week, write:

  • one moment that went well;
  • one word you modelled most;
  • one thing that seemed hard;
  • one thing you want to try next week.

Do not turn it into a data-entry job. A few lines are enough to see patterns over a month. If you use Sono, the Recents tab and Message History can support your memory, but they are not a score.

Avoid the comparison trap

Social media shows the highlight reel: the child who taps a full sentence, the adult who returns to work, the family whose device is always in use. You are not seeing the years of modelling, the bad days, or the support behind the scenes.

Compare the person to themselves last month, not to a stranger online.

Change one thing

If progress feels stuck, change one variable at a time:

  • a word that is more motivating;
  • a layout with fewer choices;
  • a time of day when the person has more energy;
  • a different communication partner;
  • a paper backup instead of the device for a while;
  • a review with a speech and language therapist.

Changing everything at once makes it impossible to know what helped.

When to ask for help

Ask for a review if:

  • weeks of daily modelling and waiting bring no response at all;
  • the person seems distressed by the board;
  • tapping is physically hard, or the symbols are hard to see;
  • you are the only person modelling, and it is wearing you down;
  • the person’s needs have changed.

Asking for help is not a sign that you failed. It is part of doing AAC well.
