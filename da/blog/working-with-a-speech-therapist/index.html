Working with a speech and language therapist on AAC

What to bring, what to ask and how to make appointments useful when you are introducing a communication system at home.

Denne artikel er endnu ikke oversat til dit sprog, så den vises på engelsk. Hjælp os med at oversætte den

Ikke medicinsk rådgivning. Denne artikel deler generel viden og erfaringer fra hverdagen. Den er ikke medicinsk, terapeutisk eller diagnostisk rådgivning. Tal med dit barns læge, logopæd eller en anden kvalificeret fagperson om beslutninger, der vedrører dit barn.

A speech and language therapist can be a useful partner when you are starting with AAC. They can assess how the person communicates, help choose vocabulary and access, and suggest what to try next. They are not the only expert in the room: you and the person who uses AAC bring the knowledge of daily life.

The relationship works best when it is a collaboration, not a set of instructions handed down.

Before the appointment

Bring a short summary, not a folder. The FAQ may answer the practical questions before the appointment.

  • how the person communicates now: speech, gestures, signs, eye pointing, a paper board, an app;
  • what has been tried, and what happened;
  • three words or routines that are going well;
  • three things that are hard;
  • any changes at school, work or home;
  • a video of a real interaction, if the person is comfortable with that.

A video can be more useful than a description. It shows the pace, the pauses and the way the person responds. Ask permission before filming, and do not share it more widely than necessary.

Questions worth asking

  • What are we trying to achieve first, and how will we know if it is working?
  • Which words or symbols should we prioritise?
  • Is the current layout and access right for this person?
  • How much modelling should we aim for, realistically?
  • What should school or other carers do differently?
  • What signs would suggest we need a different approach?
  • When should we review this?

Write the answers down. Appointments are often months apart, and memory is unreliable under pressure.

Be honest about the app

A therapist may not know Sono or may have concerns about any app. Show them what it does, including the parts you use and the parts you avoid. Be clear that it is a tool, not a therapy programme, and that you are not asking them to endorse it.

If they recommend a different system, ask why. There may be good reasons: access, vocabulary organisation, funding, or the person’s existing skills. You do not have to switch immediately, but the conversation is worth having.

Goals should fit real life

A goal such as “use the device more” is hard to measure and easy to feel bad about. A more useful goal might be:

  • “Model two core words during breakfast, four days a week.”
  • “Make sure STOP is available during personal care.”
  • “Try a choice of two at snack time and record what happens.”
  • “Set up a paper backup for school.”

Small, specific goals are easier to notice and adjust.

Bring the team together

If the person has several supporters, ask the therapist to help everyone agree on a few words and a way of waiting. A short shared plan is better than five people doing five different things. School staff, teaching assistants, carers and family members all need the same basic information.

When it is not working

If the therapist’s advice does not fit your life, say so. If the person seems distressed, or the approach is causing conflict, raise it. A good therapist will adapt. If you feel dismissed, it is reasonable to seek a second opinion or ask for a review with someone who has more AAC experience.

You are not failing by asking questions. You are doing the work of making a communication system fit a real person.
