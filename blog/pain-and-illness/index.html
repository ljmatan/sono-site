Pain and illness: helping someone describe what hurts

When someone cannot easily say where it hurts, behaviour is only part of the picture. How to prepare pain words, ask clearly and advocate in appointments.

Not medical advice. This article shares general information and everyday experience. It is not medical, therapeutic or diagnostic advice. For decisions about your child, talk to their doctor, speech and language therapist or another qualified professional.

Pain is one of the most important things a person may need to communicate, and one of the hardest. It can be sudden or persistent, sharp or dull, in a place that is hard to point to. A person who uses AAC may have limited vocabulary for pain, or may show it through behaviour that is easy to misread.

This post is general information, not medical advice. If you are worried about someone’s health, contact a doctor or emergency service.

Do not rely on behaviour alone

Changes in behaviour can be a sign of pain: withdrawing, aggression, changes in sleep or appetite, guarding a body part, crying, or becoming unusually quiet. But behaviour is not a reliable pain scale on its own, and different people show pain differently.

The goal is to combine what you observe with what the person can tell you, using every communication route available.

Prepare pain words before they are needed

In a calm moment, add words and symbols for:

  • Pain: HURT, PAIN, SORE, ACHY, SHARP, THROBBING (if useful).
  • Body parts: HEAD, TUMMY, BACK, LEG, ARM, MOUTH, EAR, TOOTH.
  • Intensity: A little / medium / a lot, or a simple scale the person understands.
  • Actions: HELP, STOP, MEDICINE, DOCTOR, HOSPITAL, SLEEP.
  • Feelings: SCARED, WORRIED, TIRED, OKAY.

A custom page for health words can keep them together without cluttering the main board. Check that the person can reach it quickly when they feel unwell.

Ask in a way that fits the person

Some people answer yes/no questions easily; others need a choice of two; others can describe in detail. Match the question to the person’s communication.

Useful questions:

  • “Does it hurt?”
  • “Show me where.”
  • “Is it a little hurt or a lot of hurt?”
  • “Does it feel hot, sharp or achy?”
  • “Do you want medicine, or do you want to wait?”
  • “Do you need the doctor?”

Give time to answer. Do not fill the pause with more questions. If the person cannot answer, say what you are observing and what you are going to do.

Believe the message

If the person says HURT or STOP, take it seriously. A person who uses AAC may have a history of their pain being dismissed because they cannot describe it in the expected way. Believing the message is the first step.

If the person’s behaviour and words seem to conflict, do not assume the words are wrong. Look for other signs, ask again later, and seek medical advice.

In appointments

Preparation makes a difference. The Out and about: parks, shops and appointments post covers appointments in more detail.

  • Bring the communication device, a charger and a paper backup.
  • Write a short note: how the person communicates, the words they use for pain, and what helps.
  • Ask the clinician to speak to the person directly and to wait for the answer.
  • Ask for yes/no questions if that is easier.
  • Make sure the person can signal STOP during an examination.

If a procedure will make communication temporarily impossible, agree in advance how the person will signal pain, stop or help. Write it down and keep it visible.

At home

Keep a simple record if pain is recurrent: when it happens, what the person says or does, what helped. This is useful for appointments and for spotting patterns. Do not turn it into a full-time data job; a few lines are enough.

A final note

Pain communication is not only a vocabulary problem. It is also a listening problem. The more often a person experiences their pain message being believed and acted on, the more likely they are to keep communicating it.
