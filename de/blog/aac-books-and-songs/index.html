Books, songs and repetition: building words through favourites

A familiar story or song gives the same words again and again. Simple ways to model symbols during reading and music without turning either into a test.

A favourite book can be read fifty times. A favourite song can be sung on every car journey. That repetition is not boring to the person who loves it; it is how language becomes predictable, and predictable moments are where communication gets easier.

Books and songs work well for AAC because they already have rhythm, repetition and a shared script. You do not need to invent a lesson. You just need to put the board nearby and model a few words.

Choose a book with a repeated line

Look for books with a phrase that comes back on every page: “We’re going on a bear hunt”, “No, David!”, “Room on the Broom”, or a simple lift-the-flap book. The repeated line becomes a chance to model the same symbols.

You can also use a wordless picture book. There is no text to follow, so you can comment on what you see: LOOK, DOG, BIG, FUNNY, UH OH.

Read it the same way twice

The first time, just read and enjoy it. The second time, add one or two symbols:

“We’re going on a bear hunt. A big one.”

Tap BEAR and BIG as you say them. Do not stop to ask the person to repeat. The story carries the language.

On later readings, pause before a familiar word and wait. If the person taps or looks, continue. If not, model it and turn the page.

Songs are built for participation

Songs have pauses built in. “If you’re happy and you know it, clap your hands” gives you a natural moment to tap HANDS and wait. “Row, row, row your boat” gives you BOAT. “The wheels on the bus go round and round” gives you GO or ROUND.

Choose one song and one word for a week. Sing it often. Model the word every time. The repetition is the point.

Do not turn the book into a quiz

It is easy to start asking, “Where’s the dog? Show me dog. What’s this?” That turns a shared story into an assessment. Instead, comment, wonder aloud and let the person join in when they want to.

If they tap a symbol that does not match the page, respond to what they said and then return to the story: “DOG! Yes, there’s a dog on the next page.” You do not have to correct every mismatch.

Make the symbols part of the ritual

Keep a small board beside the reading chair or in the car. A paper copy of the key words is enough. If you use Sono, a custom category for the current book or song can keep the relevant words together without changing the whole board.

Some families record a familiar voice for the repeated words. A grandparent’s voice saying “ALL DONE” can make the moment more meaningful.

When the person chooses

Let the person choose the book or song when they can. Offer two options and model CHOOSE, THIS or THAT. If they choose the same one again, that is a valid choice. Repetition is not a failure of imagination; it is how favourites work.

A small plan

Pick one book and one song this week. Choose three words from them. Put the symbols within reach. Read and sing as you normally would, and model those three words. Do not test. At the end of the week, notice which word the person looked at or tapped most.
