Out and about: parks, shops and appointments with AAC

A little planning makes communication easier away from home, from the playground to the GP surgery. What to pack, what to model and what to prepare.

Cet article n'est pas encore traduit dans votre langue ; il est donc affiché en anglais. Aidez-nous à le traduire

Pas un avis médical. Cet article partage des informations générales et des expériences du quotidien. Il ne constitue pas un avis médical, thérapeutique ou diagnostique. Pour toute décision concernant votre enfant, parlez-en à son médecin, à son orthophoniste ou à un autre professionnel qualifié.

Home is predictable. The board lives in the same place, the routines repeat, and the people around know how to wait. The outside world is louder, faster and less patient. That does not mean AAC stays at home.

A little planning can make days out, shopping trips and appointments much easier for everyone.

Pack for communication

Before you leave, check:

  • the device is charged, or you have a charged power bank;
  • a paper copy of the key words is in the bag;
  • the person can reach the device in the car or on the bus;
  • the case is suitable for the weather and the activity;
  • any necessary words are already on the board, not buried in a category you will have to build in a hurry.

A small printed card with the person’s main words and a short explanation can be useful at a hospital or a busy counter.

Prepare the place

If you are going somewhere new, show a photo or a symbol before you go. Name the place and what will happen:

“First the shop. Then the park.”

Model the words on the board. If the person uses a visual schedule, add the symbols to it. Predictability lowers the pressure on communication.

At the park, keep the board on a bench or in a bag you can reach. Model GO, MORE, STOP, LOOK and ALL DONE during play. At the shop, model WANT, THIS, MONEY, WAIT and ALL DONE.

Appointments

Medical appointments are high-stakes communication. It helps to prepare a short note before you go:

  • how the person communicates;
  • the words they use for pain, help, stop and yes/no;
  • what they need when they are anxious;
  • who should be spoken to, and how;
  • whether the person wants to answer questions themselves.

Show the note to the receptionist or clinician at the start. Ask them to speak directly to the person and to wait for the answer. If the person uses a device, make sure it is charged and within reach.

Do not let a clinician take the device away “for the examination” without a plan. If a procedure requires it, agree how the person will communicate during the procedure and afterwards.

Public places and privacy

A device can attract attention. Some people are happy to explain; others do not want to be a demonstration. Let the person decide how much to share. You can have a short phrase ready:

“This is how they communicate. We’re fine, thank you.”

If someone asks a question about the person, redirect it to the person: “Ask them.” This is especially important for adults and teenagers. The Emergency communication post has a backup plan for the days when the device cannot help.

When the device is not practical

The pool, the beach, a muddy walk, a crowded concert: some places are hard on devices. A laminated paper board, a keyring card or a set of picture cards can keep communication going. A backup is not a lesser form of communication; it is the same voice in a different format.

Build in a pause

The outside world rushes. You will be tempted to speak for the person to keep the queue moving. Try to build in one small pause, even three seconds, before you answer for them. If the queue is impossible, explain: “We’ll do it at the next stop.” Then follow through.

A final check

On the way home, ask yourself: where did communication happen most easily, and where did it break down? Pick one thing to change next time: a word added to the board, a paper backup, a quieter route, or a sentence prepared for a stranger.
