Emergency communication: plan beyond the app

A communication app can fail, run out of battery or be out of reach. A simple backup plan helps someone ask for help when it matters most.

Not medical advice. This article shares general information and everyday experience. It is not medical, therapeutic or diagnostic advice. For decisions about your child, talk to their doctor, speech and language therapist or another qualified professional.

Most communication happens in ordinary moments: breakfast, a joke, a request to open a window. Emergency communication is different. It is rare, high-stakes and often happens when the usual routines are disrupted.

No app should be the only way a person can call for help. A phone can run out of battery, a tablet can be dropped, an app can fail to load, and a stranger may not know what the board is. A backup plan is not a sign of distrust in technology; it is part of caring for someone.

Start with the basics

Make sure the person has more than one way to communicate an emergency:

  • a charged phone or tablet with the communication app;
  • a paper card with key phrases and emergency contacts;
  • a medical ID or wallet card, if appropriate;
  • a trusted person who knows how the person communicates;
  • a way to make a loud noise or attract attention, if the person can use one.

Keep the paper card where it will be found: in a wallet, school bag, wheelchair bag or on the fridge. Laminate it if it will get wet.

What goes on the card

Keep it short and practical:

  • “I use a communication app / board to speak.”
  • “I may need more time to answer.”
  • “Please ask me yes/no questions if you need to.”
  • “Call [name] on [number].”
  • Any essential medical information the person wants to share.
  • A few key symbols or words: HELP, PAIN, STOP, YES, NO.

Do not include private information that is not needed in an emergency. The person should decide what goes on the card, if they can.

Practise in calm moments

A plan nobody has tried is only a hope. In a calm moment, show the person where the card is and what it says. Practise a simple scenario:

“If you needed help and the tablet wasn’t working, what could you do?”

Model the words HELP and EMERGENCY on the board. Make sure the person knows they can point to the card, show it to a stranger, or press a phone button.

If the person uses a device, check that the emergency words are not buried in a subcategory that is hard to reach when stressed. A dedicated page or a Quick Phrase can help.

Teach the people around them

Teachers, carers, grandparents and friends should know:

  • how the person communicates;
  • where the backup card is;
  • how to ask a simple yes/no question;
  • who to call;
  • that the person may need extra time.

A short note in the school file or a message to a regular carer is enough. It does not need to be a formal plan unless the person’s needs are complex.

At hospital

Hospital appointments and admissions are a common emergency-adjacent situation. Bring the communication device, a charger, the paper backup and a short note about how the person communicates. Tell the staff at reception. Ask them to speak to the person directly.

If a procedure will make communication temporarily impossible, agree in advance how the person will signal stop, pain or help. Write it down.

After the emergency

When everyone is safe, review what worked. Did the device have battery? Was the card findable? Did the person know what to do? Change one thing. A plan improves through small, honest revisions, not through a perfect document written once.

A final note

This post is general information, not a personal emergency plan. If the person has complex medical needs, ask their doctor, therapist or emergency service for advice. The goal is simple: more than one way to be heard, and people around who know how to listen.
