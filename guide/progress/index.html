Usage guide

Seeing progress

What early progress looks like, how to notice it, how long to give it and when to change something. Every person moves at their own pace, so compare the person with how they were a few weeks ago, not with anyone else.

On this page

  1. What early progress looks like
  2. Keep a simple note each week
  3. Give it months, not days
  4. When to change words or layout
  5. Review goals with a professional

What early progress looks like # — Copy link to this section

Early progress rarely looks like a sentence. It usually starts small, and much of it happens before the person taps anything on their own. In a small study) of two preschool children, an adult modelled with symbols: both children came to understand more symbols and, when asked, used more of them, but for both of them using the symbols lagged behind understanding them.

Professional guidance counts every way of communicating, not just taps. ASHA, the American Speech-Language-Hearing Association, describes good practice as combining several ways, so that the person is never limited to either a device or their body. Speech Pathology Australia, the professional association for speech pathologists in Australia, calls using more than one way multimodal communication. The Communication Matrix, an assessment from Oregon Health & Science University, describes a similar path: from movements and sounds that aren't yet used to communicate, through gestures such as pointing and waving, to pictures and other symbols, and later two or three symbols together.

Signs worth noticing

  • Looking: the person glances at the board when you tap, or follows your finger.
  • Reaching: they reach for the device, pull it closer or push your hand towards it.
  • Tapping in routines: a tap at the same moment each day, such as MORE at snack time, even when it isn't the word you expected.
  • Combining: a tap together with a look, a gesture or a sound, such as STOP while pushing your hand away.
  • Copying: they tap the word you have just modelled.
  • Starting: they tap without being asked, to get your attention or to start a game.
  • New reasons: besides asking for things, they refuse, greet, comment or ask questions.

Each of these counts, and not every person shows them all or in this order. Write them down, and answer each one as Give time, then answer every attempt describes.

  • Communication Matrix (Design to Learn, Oregon Health & Science University) — Research basis: seven levels and four reasons to communicate
  • AssistiveWare — Communication partner skills for AAC learners

Keep a simple note each week # — Copy link to this section

Progress is hard to see from one day to the next and much easier across a month, but only if something was written down. A few lines a week are enough. AssistiveWare, the makers of another communication app, suggest taking photos and videos of good moments to share with the rest of the team. An article for speech and language professionals recommends noting what the partners do as well: how often they modelled, and how many real chances the person had to communicate. Without that, it is easy to miss why progress has slowed.

How to do it in Sono

  1. Once a week, write down the date, the words you modelled most, the routines you used them in, what the person did (looked, reached, tapped, combined, started) and one moment worth remembering.
  2. Open Caretaker Settings > Message History. It keeps the last 20 sentences spoken with Speak from the sentence strip, newest first, and Show More reveals more of them. There are no dates, so copy anything worth keeping into your note.
  3. Single words spoken with one tap don't go into Message History. In the Advanced and Simple layouts, look at the Recents tab on the board instead: it keeps the last 30 different symbols used, newest first. A word you have never modelled turning up there is worth noting.
  4. Both lists include your own taps and sentences, so your note is what tells the person's words apart from your modelling.
  5. The Simple layout has no sentence strip, so nothing new reaches Message History while it is in use, and the Menu layout has no Recents tab. Your note covers both gaps.
  6. Now and then, film a short everyday moment. Keep the videos private and share them only with the people who support the person.

Sources

  • AssistiveWare — Don't give up too soon
  • PrAACtical AAC (Zangari, 2017) — Data collection for the beginning AAC user: essential things to measure to expedite progress

Give it months, not days # — Copy link to this section

Learning to communicate with symbols means learning a new language, and AssistiveWare, the makers of another communication app, stress that this takes practice and time. They quote an estimate by Jane Korsten, a speech-language pathologist: by the time a typical toddler starts speaking, at about 18 months, they have heard spoken language for around 4,380 waking hours. Someone who sees their symbols used only twice a week, for 20 to 30 minutes each time, would need about 84 years to get the same experience. That is one reason everyday modelling at home matters so much, as Interest grows from seeing it used explains.

Professional guidance treats this as a long process. ASHA, the American Speech-Language-Hearing Association, describes assessment as an ongoing process, and notes that how a system is organised may change over time as the person's skills and situations change. An article for speech and language professionals points out that progress often slows down or even stalls after early successes, and suggests looking at how much modelling and how many chances to communicate the person is getting.

What to do

  1. Give each new step, such as a new layout or a new set of words, a few weeks of daily use before you judge it.
  2. Compare the person with themselves: this month's notes against last month's, not against other people.
  3. Expect quiet weeks. When progress slows, look at the modelling and the chances to communicate first, before deciding that the app or the person isn't ready.
  4. Celebrate small steps out loud, and tell the rest of the team about them.
  5. If weeks of daily modelling bring no reaction of any kind, or the board seems to upset the person, don't wait: Every person is unique: a professional can help lists when to ask for help sooner.

Sources

  • AssistiveWare — Don't give up too soon
  • ASHA — Augmentative and Alternative Communication (Practice Portal)
  • PrAACtical AAC (Zangari, 2017) — Data collection for the beginning AAC user: essential things to measure to expedite progress

When to change words or layout # — Copy link to this section

A board that changes too often is hard to learn, and one that never changes runs out of words. Professional guidance points both ways. ASHA, the American Speech-Language-Hearing Association, notes that how a system is organised may change as skills and situations change, and also that keeping words in the same place lowers the demands on memory and on motor planning. AssistiveWare, the makers of another communication app, add that words kept in consistent places help the person find them quickly.

It may be time to add words when

  • the person uses the current words often and easily,
  • they point at, bring you or try to show you something that isn't on the board,
  • you keep wanting to model a word you can't find.

It may be time to change the size or the layout when

  • taps often land on the wrong symbol, or the person tires quickly: try fewer columns, Circle tiles or the Menu layout,
  • the person starts tapping two words in a row and looking at you: the Advanced and Menu layouts have a sentence strip to join them.

How to do it in Sono

  1. Add words rather than rearranging the board. Put new photos in Caretaker Settings > My Symbols, and bring back hidden words under Caretaker Settings > Hidden Symbols.
  2. Know what moves things. A new Grid Size or layout puts symbols in new places, and hiding or showing a symbol shifts the ones after it. Recents re-sorts itself with every tap, so words don't keep a fixed place there.
  3. Change one thing at a time, write the date in your weekly note and give it a few weeks.
  4. Show everyone who models where the new words are.
  5. Talk bigger changes, such as a new layout, through with the person's speech and language therapist first.

Sources

  • ASHA — Augmentative and Alternative Communication (Practice Portal)
  • AssistiveWare (2019) — Templates

Review goals with a professional # — Copy link to this section

Your notes are most useful in the hands of someone who can help decide what comes next. ASHA, the American Speech-Language-Hearing Association, describes assessment as ongoing: the therapist documents progress and decides what to change in the system. Speech Pathology Australia, the professional association for speech pathologists in Australia, adds that speech pathologists help people get more out of the system they already use and teach family and friends ways to help.

The Communication Matrix, from Oregon Health & Science University, is a free online assessment for people of any age at the earliest stages of communication, made to help families as well as professionals see where a person is and how they are progressing. It marks each skill as emerging (done now and then, or only when prompted) or mastered (done on their own most of the time). Fees apply only to those who use it more than five times in twelve months.

What to bring to a review

  • your weekly notes and a few short videos,
  • a screenshot of Message History and of the Recents tab,
  • the words you are modelling now, and the ones you would like to add.

Questions worth asking

  1. What is the next step for this person, and how will we notice it?
  2. Which words should we model next, and in which routines?
  3. Are the layout and Grid Size right, or should an occupational therapist look at how they reach and tap?
  4. What should school and other carers do the same way?

Aim for goals about communicating, not about using an app. The Communication Matrix sorts communication into four reasons: refusing, getting things, social contact, and giving or asking for information, so a plan can cover more than requests. Every person is different, so the right goals are the ones agreed with the person, their family and their therapist.
