More than “more”: comments, feelings and jokes with AAC

Requesting is only one reason to communicate. Comments, feelings, protests and silly moments are what make a voice feel like a person.

Ovaj članak još nije preveden na tvoj jezik pa je prikazan na engleskom. Pomozi nam ga prevesti

Nije medicinski savjet. Ovaj članak donosi opće informacije i iskustva iz svakodnevice. Nije medicinski, terapijski ni dijagnostički savjet. O odlukama koje se tiču vašeg djeteta razgovarajte s liječnikom, logopedom ili drugim kvalificiranim stručnjakom.

Many symbol boards are organised around what a person might want. That is a sensible place to start, but it can leave someone able to ask for a biscuit and little else. A voice that can only request is a very practical voice and a very lonely one.

Communication has many jobs. We comment on what we see, complain, joke, greet, ask questions, tell stories and share feelings. A good AAC plan makes room for all of it. The Small words, big difference post explains the core vocabulary that makes those functions possible.

The functions we forget

Think about a recent conversation with someone you love. How much of it was requesting? Probably very little. Now look at the board with these functions in mind:

  • Commenting: LOOK, FUNNY, WOW, UH OH, PRETTY.
  • Feelings: HAPPY, SAD, TIRED, SCARED, EXCITED, ANGRY.
  • Social: HI, BYE, THANK YOU, PLEASE, SORRY, MY TURN.
  • Protesting: NO, STOP, ALL DONE, NOT.
  • Asking: WHAT, WHERE, WHO, WHY.
  • Joking and playing: SILLY, AGAIN, BOO, YUM, YUCK.

If those words are not on the board, or are hidden deep inside categories, the person cannot easily use them.

Model the words you want to hear

The most effective way to bring a new function into use is to use it yourself. During a shared activity, make a comment rather than asking a question:

“LOOK! A dog.”

“Uh oh, it fell.”

“That tickles. FUNNY.”

Tap one or two words as you say them. Do not ask the person to repeat. You are showing that the board is for comments, not just for getting things.

Feelings need more than one word

A single HAPPY symbol is not enough for a real emotional life. Try to include:

  • different intensities: OKAY, GOOD, GREAT, TOO MUCH;
  • uncomfortable feelings: HURT, SCARED, WORRIED;
  • physical states: TIRED, HUNGRY, HOT, COLD;
  • a way to say “I don’t know” or “I need a minute”.

Model feelings about yourself too: “I’m tired. I need a cup of tea.” Children learn emotional vocabulary by hearing it used honestly around them.

Make room for silly

Humour is not a frill. It is how people connect. Add a few words that are simply fun: YUCK, POO (if that is the household humour), SILLY, BOING, WOW. Use them in play. A board that can make someone laugh is a board that gets picked up again.

A small planning exercise

Choose one routine this week, such as bath time or the walk to school. Write down three comments, one feeling and one protest that fit that routine. Put the matching symbols in easy reach, or add them as custom symbols. Model them yourself for a week. Do not test. At the end of the week, notice which ones the person picked up or looked at.

Keep the balance

Requests will probably come first because they get quick, visible results. That is fine. The aim is not to remove requesting but to widen the conversation. A person who can say “more”, “stop”, “look” and “silly” has a much richer way to be with other people.
