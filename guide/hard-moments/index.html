Usage guide

Calm ways through hard moments

Every child and adult has hard moments. Here is how Sono can give the person a quicker way to say stop, no or help, and how to support them before and after. This is general guidance, not a behaviour plan: every person is different, and behaviour that is risky or keeps getting worse needs professionals who know the person.

On this page

  1. Behaviour is often a message
  2. Make stop, no and help quick to reach
  3. Model in the calm moments
  4. Keep their voice with them
  5. Getting the right support

Behaviour is often a message # — Copy link to this section

Screaming, hitting, throwing things or running off can be the fastest way a person has to say "stop", "too loud", "help me", "it hurts" or "I want that". A tip sheet on crises from the Vanderbilt Kennedy Center, a US university centre for developmental disabilities, puts it plainly: behaviour communicates unmet needs, and in a crisis it is a stress response, not wilful misbehaviour.

NICE, the UK's National Institute for Health and Care Excellence, lists communication difficulties among the things that make behaviour that challenges more likely in people with learning disabilities (the UK term for intellectual disabilities). It recommends finding out what the behaviour achieves for the person and helping them learn another way to achieve the same thing, for example better communication. ASHA, the American Speech-Language-Hearing Association, also notes that everyday communication skills, such as asking for things and protesting, help reduce challenging behaviour. Carole Zangari, a professor of speech and language therapy, adds a catch: the new way has to work as well as the old one and take no more effort.

What to do

  1. For a week, jot down what happens just before a difficult moment, where and when it happens, and what happens straight after.
  2. Ask what the behaviour might be saying: stop, a break, help, more, that hurts.
  3. Find that message on the board and make it quick to reach.
  4. Answer the symbol at least as fast as you would answer the behaviour. If tapping STOP works more slowly than screaming, screaming stays the better tool.
  5. If the behaviour is new or has changed suddenly, ask a doctor to check for pain or illness. NICE asks for new or suddenly changed behaviour to be looked at together with any changes in the person's health and surroundings.

Sources

  • Vanderbilt Kennedy Center — PAUSE: managing behavioral or emotional crises in students with extensive support needs (PDF)
  • NICE (2015) — Challenging behaviour and learning disabilities: prevention and interventions for people with learning disabilities whose behaviour challenges (NG11)
  • ASHA — Augmentative and Alternative Communication (Practice Portal)
  • PrAACtical AAC (Zangari, 2014) — On communication and behavior: back to basics
  • Vanderbilt Kennedy Center TRIAD — Autism and behavior: AAC supports and strategies (PDF)

Make stop, no and help quick to reach # — Copy link to this section

Being able to refuse matters. The Communication Bill of Rights, drawn up by a joint committee of US professional associations, includes the right to ask for or refuse objects, actions, events and people, and the right to a response to all communication, even when the person can't have what they asked for. A tip sheet from the US Vanderbilt Kennedy Center adds that protesting and asking for help are essential for speaking up for oneself, and that a way to ask for a break should always be easy to reach.

How to do it in Sono

  1. In the Advanced and Menu layouts, the Quick button (the lightning bolt) on the empty sentence strip opens a row of phrases that are spoken straight away, among them Stop, No, Help and Wait.
  2. Add ALL DONE to that row under Caretaker Settings > Quick Phrases > Add from Library. Switch off the phrases the person doesn't use, so the row stays short.
  3. The Simple layout has no Quick button. There, STOP, HELP and ALL DONE are in Basics and NO is in Manners. Hiding what the person doesn't need yet under Caretaker Settings > Hidden Symbols moves these words closer to the top of their tab.
  4. For a break, make your own card: Caretaker Settings > My Symbols, tap +, take a photo of their quiet spot, name it "Break" and tap Create. It appears on the My Symbols tab.
  5. Honour the word straight away whenever it is safe. When it isn't, still answer. For example:
    • They tap: STOP, during tickles. You say: "Stop? Okay, I'm stopping." And you stop.
    • They tap: NO, about the seatbelt. You say: "You said no. The seatbelt stays on, we're nearly home." You tap: HOME.
  6. Print Basics for times without the device: Caretaker Settings > Print Boards, choose Basics, then Share PDF.

Sources

  • NJC / ASHA — Communication Bill of Rights, 3rd edition
  • Vanderbilt Kennedy Center TRIAD — Autism and behavior: AAC supports and strategies (PDF)

Model in the calm moments # — Copy link to this section

In a meltdown, stress limits how much language a person can take in. The Vanderbilt Kennedy Center, a US university centre for developmental disabilities, advises putting safety first, speaking less, using short phrases of three or four words and offering no more than two choices; talking about what happened comes later, once everyone is calm. Carole Zangari, a professor of speech and language therapy, notes that once someone is upset it is practically impossible to teach anything new, so new ways to communicate are best taught at calm times, with lots of practice.

What to do

  1. Before: in calm moments and in play, model the words for hard moments: STOP, HELP, ALL DONE, WAIT. AssistiveWare, which makes communication apps, suggests naming a feeling when you think the person is feeling it, and naming your own feelings too. For example:
    • You say: "I can't find my keys. I feel so frustrated!" You tap: FRUSTRATED.
    • You say: "It's too loud in here. Let's find somewhere quiet." You tap: LOUD, then QUIET.
  2. During: keep the device within reach, but don't ask for taps, don't ask questions and don't say "show me on the tablet". At most, calmly tap one word yourself, such as STOP, HELP or ALL DONE.
  3. Keep your voice low and your words few, and make the room calmer: less noise, fewer people.
  4. After: once the person is calm again, keep it short. Name what happened in a word or two, then move on. For example:
    • You say: "You were angry. It was so loud." You tap: ANGRY, then LOUD.
  5. In the next calm moment, model the word that could have helped, during a game or an everyday routine.

Sources

  • Vanderbilt Kennedy Center — PAUSE: managing behavioral or emotional crises in students with extensive support needs (PDF)
  • Vanderbilt Kennedy Center TRIAD — Autism and behavior: AAC supports and strategies (PDF)
  • PrAACtical AAC (Zangari, 2014) — On communication and behavior: back to basics
  • AssistiveWare (2019) — Using AAC to talk about emotions

Keep their voice with them # — Copy link to this section

Taking the device away after a meltdown can feel like a fair consequence, but it takes away the person's voice when they may need it most. ISAAC, the International Society for Augmentative and Alternative Communication, says a person's communication system should never be taken away, and the Communication Bill of Rights includes a working communication system at all times. Carole Zangari, a professor of speech and language therapy, is just as clear: switching the device off, taking it away or muting it is not an option, because silencing a person by restricting their access to it is never okay. A tip sheet on crises from the Vanderbilt Kennedy Center lists communication aids among the supports that can help a person calm down, and reminds adults that support is a need, not a reward.

What to do

  1. Don't use Sono as a reward or take it away as a punishment, and ask everyone around the person to do the same.
  2. If the device itself is about to be thrown, move it just out of harm's way and offer it back as soon as the person is calmer.
  3. Protect it rather than remove it: a sturdy case, a screen protector, a strap or a stand.
  4. For someone new to the board, tapping the same word again and again can be part of exploring and learning it. Answer it, or let it be, but don't mute it.

For keeping Sono charged, awake and with the person all day, and for a paper copy when the device can't come along, see Keep it within reach, all day.

Getting the right support # — Copy link to this section

Some hard moments fade as the person finds better ways to be understood. Others need people who can look at the whole picture. NICE, the UK's National Institute for Health and Care Excellence, has guidance for people with learning disabilities whose behaviour challenges. It recommends an assessment that looks at the person's communication, physical and mental health, medication, surroundings and routines, and at what the behaviour achieves for them, followed by a written support plan. It says families should be fully involved in the assessment and the plan, and told about the support available to them as carers.

Ask for help soon if

  • the behaviour puts the person or anyone else at risk of harm,
  • it is getting more frequent or more intense, or starts suddenly,
  • it keeps the person from school, outings or everyday life,
  • you feel worn out or unsure what to do next.

Who can help, depending on where you live

  • A family doctor or paediatrician, to look for pain, illness or side effects of medication.
  • A speech and language therapist, to look at how the person communicates and what they need to be able to say.
  • A behaviour support specialist, such as a clinical psychologist or a behaviour analyst, to work out what the behaviour is for and plan support with you.

Bring your notes on when and where things happen, and show them how the person uses Sono. If anyone is in immediate danger, call your local emergency number: Sono is not meant for emergencies.
