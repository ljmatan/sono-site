Siblings and AAC: making room for everyone's voice

Brothers and sisters are often the best communication partners, and sometimes the most overlooked. How to include them without making them carers.

Besprich Entscheidungen für dein Kind mit der Kinderärztin oder dem Kinderarzt, der Logopädin oder dem Logopäden oder einer anderen qualifizierten Fachperson.

Siblings grow up close to AAC. They see the device on the kitchen table, watch the modelling, and often understand their brother or sister faster than any adult. They also have their own needs, their own friendships and their own reasons to feel overlooked.

A good AAC plan includes siblings, but it does not make them responsible for communication.

What siblings may be feeling

Brothers and sisters can feel proud, protective, resentful, embarrassed, curious or exhausted, sometimes all in one day. Common experiences include:

  • being asked to interpret or translate for their sibling;
  • feeling that the device gets more attention than they do;
  • worrying that they should always be patient;
  • being told they are “so good” for helping, which can make it hard to admit they don’t want to;
  • wanting to explain AAC to friends but not knowing how.

None of these feelings are a problem to be fixed. They are normal responses to a family situation that asks a lot.

Give them a simple explanation

Siblings usually need less detail than adults think. A short explanation is enough:

“This is how Alex talks. The board says words out loud. You can talk to Alex normally. If you don’t understand, wait, or ask Alex to show you again.”

Let them ask questions. Some will want to know why their sibling doesn’t speak; some will not care. Follow their lead.

Invite them into modelling

Siblings can be wonderful models because they are natural, silly and fast. Show them one or two words and let them try during a game. Do not correct their technique in front of the person who uses AAC. Keep it playful.

A sibling who models STOP during a tickle game is not doing therapy. They are being a brother or sister. That is exactly the point.

Do not make them the interpreter

It is tempting to rely on the sibling who “just knows”. But constant interpreting is a burden, and it can keep the person who uses AAC from developing their own voice with other people.

If a sibling often understands before the device does, acknowledge it and then give the person a chance to communicate for themselves. The sibling can still help, but not as a permanent translator.

Protect one-to-one time

A device brings appointments, school meetings and daily practice. Siblings notice. Try to protect a small amount of time that belongs only to them, without the device and without a therapy goal. It does not need to be long. A walk, a game, or reading together can be enough.

This is not about making things equal every minute. It is about making sure each child is seen.

Let them be honest

Give siblings permission to say “I don’t want to help right now” or “I wish things were easier.” Listen without correcting. A child who can be honest about the hard parts is more likely to stay kind in the long run.

If a sibling is struggling, a family therapist or a support group for siblings can help. You do not have to solve every feeling at the kitchen table.

A small family habit

Once a week, show each other one thing that worked. It might be a new word, a funny tap, a moment of patience, or a game that went well. Keep it short. The aim is to notice progress together, not to grade anyone.
