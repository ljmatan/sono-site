No, stop, help: teaching control and consent with AAC

A communication system is not only for asking. The words that let someone refuse, change their mind and ask for help matter just as much.

Tento článok zatiaľ nie je preložený do vášho jazyka, preto sa zobrazuje v angličtine. Pomôžte nám ho preložiť

Nejde o lekársku radu. Tento článok prináša všeobecné informácie a skúsenosti z bežného života. Nejde o lekársku, terapeutickú ani diagnostickú radu. O rozhodnutiach týkajúcich sa vášho dieťaťa sa poraďte s jeho lekárom, logopédom alebo iným kvalifikovaným odborníkom.

A symbol board can look like a menu of requests: juice, biscuit, bubbles, again. Requests are useful. But if that is all a person can say, they have a very small voice. Communication is also how we say no, set a boundary, ask for help, and change the subject.

The words for control are often the ones that change daily life most.

Make refusal easy to reach

Check the board together with the person, if they can take part. Where are NO, STOP, ALL DONE and HELP? Are they in the first place a hand reaches, or buried three categories deep?

  • Keep at least one clear “stop” word on the main page.
  • Put HELP somewhere predictable and easy to find.
  • Make sure a “finished” word is available during meals, activities and personal care.
  • If the person uses a sentence strip, check that a single tap can still produce a quick no.

In Sono, Quick Phrases can speak short messages with one tap. Decide together which phrases belong there. A phrase that is only useful to the adult is not the priority; a phrase that lets the person end something is.

Honour the no

A “no” only becomes meaningful when it works. That does not mean every no can be granted: teeth still need brushing and medicine still needs taking. It does mean noticing the no, acknowledging it, and explaining what will happen next.

“You said STOP. I’m stopping the tickle. We can do it again later.”

“I hear NO. We still need to change your nappy. I’ll be quick, and then you can choose a book.”

When a no cannot be followed, say so plainly and gently. Do not pretend you did not hear it. The goal is not obedience; it is a person who learns that their communication has power.

Teach yes as well as no

Consent is not only refusal. Offer real choices and accept the answer:

  • Two snacks, two books, two songs.
  • “This one or that one?”
  • “Do you want help, or do you want to try?”

Avoid asking a question when there is only one acceptable answer. If the person must do something, say what is happening rather than dressing it up as a choice.

Model boundaries in everyday talk

Children and adults learn from hearing other people set boundaries too. Model your own:

  • “I’m tired. I need a break.”
  • “Not now, later.”
  • “That’s too loud. Quieter, please.”
  • “I don’t like that. Stop.”

These are not lectures. They are short comments during real moments, with the matching symbols tapped on the board.

Keep the device available

Taking a communication device away as a punishment removes the person’s voice, not just a toy. The Communication Bill of Rights calls for a working communication system at all times. If a device is being used to say something rude or to get attention, treat that as communication to be understood and worked with, not as a reason to remove it.

If the device itself is being thrown or used to hurt someone, the safety plan may need to include a pause and a paper backup. That is different from taking communication away for the rest of the day.

A small script for the hard moments

  1. Notice the message, even if it is a tap, a look or a behaviour.
  2. Name it simply: “You want this to stop.”
  3. Say what you can do: “I can stop for a minute. Then we need to finish.”
  4. Offer a next step: “Do you want a break or a hug?”
  5. Follow through, and come back to the board when everyone is calm.

Control is not a reward for good behaviour. It is part of communication. The more often a person experiences their no being heard, the more they can trust that their yes, their help and their comments will be heard too.
