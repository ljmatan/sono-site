Signs, gestures and symbols: a total communication approach

AAC is not one device or one method. Speech, signs, gestures, pointing and symbols can work together, and accepting every route makes communication easier.

Not medical advice. This article shares general information and everyday experience. It is not medical, therapeutic or diagnostic advice. For decisions about your child, talk to their doctor, speech and language therapist or another qualified professional.

A common worry is that using symbols will stop someone learning to speak, or that signing will replace a device. In practice, communication is rarely a competition between methods. People use speech, gestures, facial expressions, pointing, signs, writing and symbols together, often in the same sentence.

This is sometimes called total communication. The idea is simple: accept and support every way a person can get a message across, and add tools rather than removing them.

Every attempt counts

A look, a reach, a sound, a point, a sign, a tap or a behaviour can all be communication. The first job of a communication partner is to notice the attempt and respond to it.

That does not mean guessing wildly. It means treating the attempt as meaningful, then modelling the word that fits:

“You’re looking at the door. OUTSIDE? Yes, let’s go outside.”

If the person signs “more” and also has a device, accept the sign and model MORE on the device. You are not correcting them. You are adding another route.

Methods can support each other

  • Speech gives the person a model of the word and a way to be understood by people who do not know the board.
  • Signs and gestures are fast, portable and work when the device is out of reach.
  • Symbols and photos make abstract words visible and give a reliable shared vocabulary.
  • A device speaks for the person and can produce sentences, not just single words.
  • Writing and typing give access to spelling for people who can use it.

A person may use a sign at home, a paper board at school and an app in the community. That is not inconsistency. It is a toolkit.

Do not police the method

Phrases such as “use your talker” or “we don’t sign here” can teach a person that one way of communicating is unacceptable. Unless a specific method is unsafe or confusing in a particular setting, let the person choose the fastest route.

There are exceptions. A school may need a consistent system for a specific activity, or a device may be needed to build sentence length. Even then, the rule should be explained and temporary, not a blanket ban.

Model across methods

When you speak, you can point to a symbol, make a sign and say the word at the same time. This gives the person several chances to catch the meaning.

For example, at the door:

  • say “outside”;
  • point to OUTSIDE on the board;
  • make the sign for “outside” if you know it;
  • open the door.

The goal is not to perform four things perfectly. It is to make the word visible in more than one way.

Partners need to know the routes

A total communication approach only works if the people around know what the person uses. A short profile can help:

  • “Alex uses a few signs, a paper board and an app. Alex understands spoken English. Please give extra time and accept a point or a look as an answer.”

Update it when the person’s methods change. Share it with school, carers and relatives. The Explaining AAC to grandparents and relatives post has a simple script.

When methods conflict

Sometimes a person uses a sign that only family understands, or a device that a new carer cannot operate. The answer is not to remove the method. It is to build shared understanding:

  • teach the key signs to the people who need them;
  • keep a paper copy of the device board;
  • agree on a small set of core words across settings;
  • ask a speech and language therapist for help if the team is stuck.

A note on speech

Using AAC does not mean giving up on speech. Some people develop speech alongside AAC; others do not. Either way, the communication system should be available. Speech is one outcome, not a prerequisite for being heard.

A small change this week

Pick one word and model it in three ways: say it, point to it, and make a gesture or sign. Do this in a real routine, not a lesson. Notice which route the person responds to. Then keep offering all three.
