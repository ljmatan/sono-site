Small words, big difference: why core words matter

Words like I, want, stop, go and help do more work than a hundred nouns. How to choose and model core vocabulary without turning it into a lesson.

Cet article n'est pas encore traduit dans votre langue ; il est donc affiché en anglais. Aidez-nous à le traduire

Pas un avis médical. Cet article partage des informations générales et des expériences du quotidien. Il ne constitue pas un avis médical, thérapeutique ou diagnostique. Pour toute décision concernant votre enfant, parlez-en à son médecin, à son orthophoniste ou à un autre professionnel qualifié.

A board full of pictures can still be hard to speak with. If every symbol is a specific thing, a person can ask for an apple but cannot say “I don’t want it”, “more”, “stop” or “help me”. The small, ordinary words are what turn a picture menu into a language.

Speech and language therapists often call these core words. They are the words we use constantly, across almost every situation.

What counts as a core word

Core words are usually short and reusable: I, you, it, want, like, more, all done, stop, go, help, look, what, where, not, my, your. They are not tied to one topic. The same word works at breakfast, in the bath, at school and in the car.

Fringe words are the specific ones: apple, dinosaur, grandma, bus, swimming. A person needs both. Core words build the sentence; fringe words make it personal.

A useful rule of thumb is to keep a small set of core words visible on the main page and let the specific words live in categories. That way the person does not have to navigate three levels deep to say “stop”.

Start with a handful, not a dictionary

You do not need to introduce every core word at once. Choose six to ten that fit the person’s day. A common first set is:

  • I / ME
  • YOU
  • WANT
  • MORE
  • STOP / NO
  • GO
  • HELP
  • LOOK
  • ALL DONE
  • LIKE

If the person already has favourite words, keep them. A board that ignores what someone loves will not get used, no matter how well organised it is.

Keep the position stable

Once a word has a place, try not to move it around every week. A person who is learning to find STOP should be able to reach for the same place each time, even as other symbols change. Stability supports motor memory, especially for people who find precise pointing difficult.

If you need to add a word, add it in a predictable place rather than rearranging the whole board.

Model core words everywhere

The point of core words is that they work in many situations. Model them during ordinary talk:

  • At breakfast: WANT, MORE, ALL DONE.
  • Getting dressed: GO, HELP, MY.
  • Play: LOOK, AGAIN, STOP.
  • Outdoors: GO, WHERE, LOOK.
  • A difficult moment: STOP, HELP, NO.

Tap one or two words as you speak. You are not testing. You are showing how the same small word fits different moments.

Build sentences slowly

Once single words are familiar, model two-word combinations:

  • WANT MORE
  • GO OUTSIDE
  • LOOK DOG
  • HELP ME
  • STOP THAT
  • I LIKE

In Sono’s Advanced and Menu layouts, two taps on the sentence strip can model a short phrase; in Simple, each tap speaks one word. Choose the layout that matches where the person is now, not where you hope they will be next month.

A note about nouns

It is easy to fill a board with nouns because nouns are easy to picture. But a board with only nouns puts the person in the position of naming things. Core words give them a way to act, refuse, comment and ask. Keep the nouns, but do not let them crowd out the small words.
