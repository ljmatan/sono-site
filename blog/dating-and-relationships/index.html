Dating and relationships with AAC: love, consent and boundaries

Adults and teenagers who use AAC have the same right to relationships as anyone else. How to make room for dating, privacy and clear consent.

Not medical advice. This article shares general information and everyday experience. It is not medical, therapeutic or diagnostic advice. For decisions about your child, talk to their doctor, speech and language therapist or another qualified professional.

Dating is a normal part of adult life. People who use AAC fall in love, flirt, build relationships and make decisions about their bodies and futures. Too often, those parts of life are treated as somehow separate from AAC, or as something to be managed by carers rather than lived by the person.

This post is general information, not a substitute for relationship or sexual health advice. It is about making sure the communication system supports the whole person.

Start from rights, not protection

Adults have the right to:

  • choose whether to date;
  • decide who to be with;
  • say yes, no or not yet;
  • keep their relationship private;
  • marry or not marry;
  • have or not have children;
  • change their mind.

A communication device should make those decisions easier to express, not harder. If the people around the person make every relationship decision for them, the device is not really a voice.

Build the vocabulary

A board built only for school or therapy may have no words for love, attraction or boundaries. Add:

  • Feelings: LIKE, LOVE, CRUSH, HAPPY, NERVOUS, EXCITED.
  • Relationship words: FRIEND, PARTNER, DATE, BOYFRIEND, GIRLFRIEND, TOGETHER.
  • Boundaries: NO, STOP, SLOW DOWN, NOT YET, YES, I WANT.
  • Privacy: PRIVATE, LATER, NOT HERE, MY BUSINESS.
  • Health: SAFE, CONDOM, TEST, DOCTOR (if the person wants these words).
  • Communication: TEXT, CALL, MEET, TALK.

Keep the words where the person can reach them without asking permission. Privacy matters.

Consent is communication

Consent is not a single word. It is an ongoing conversation, and it needs a communication system that can express yes, no, uncertainty and change of mind.

Practise phrases such as:

  • “I want this.”
  • “Not yet.”
  • “Stop.”
  • “I need to think.”
  • “I don’t want to.”
  • “I’m not sure.”

If the person cannot use spoken words, their AAC system and their body language both matter. Partners should know how the person signals yes and no, and should never assume silence means agreement.

Disclosure is a personal choice

Whether to explain AAC on a date is up to the person. Some people explain early; others prefer to let the device speak for itself. There is no right answer.

If a partner is curious, a short explanation is enough: “This is how I talk. You can ask me questions directly.” The partner should talk to the person, not only to the carer or interpreter. The Privacy and dignity post has more on sharing information and protecting personal space.

Support without taking over

Families and carers can help by:

  • making sure the device is charged and available;
  • respecting privacy when the person is with a partner;
  • not interrogating the person about a date;
  • helping with transport or logistics if asked;
  • stepping back when the person can make their own decision.

Support should increase the person’s control, not replace it.

Safety and abuse

People with disabilities are not immune to abuse. A person who uses AAC needs words and allies to recognise and report it. This includes:

  • pressure to do something sexual;
  • having money or possessions taken;
  • being touched without consent;
  • being isolated from friends;
  • threats or humiliation.

If the person reports abuse, believe them and help them access appropriate support. Do not dismiss it because of their communication or disability. Safeguarding procedures should be followed, and the person should be involved in decisions about what happens next.

A final thought

Love and relationships are not an extra. They are part of a full life. A communication system that can say “I love you”, “not tonight” and “this is private” is not a luxury. It is part of being an adult.
