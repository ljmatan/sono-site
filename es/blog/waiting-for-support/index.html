Waiting for an assessment or funding: what you can do meanwhile

Waiting lists can be long, and starting AAC should not depend on a perfect plan. Practical steps to take while you wait for professional or financial support.

No es consejo médico. Este artículo comparte información general y experiencias del día a día. No es un consejo médico, terapéutico ni diagnóstico. Para las decisiones sobre tu hijo o hija, habla con su médico, su logopeda u otro profesional cualificado.

Waiting for an assessment, a therapist appointment or funding for a communication system can take months. In that time, a person still needs to communicate. The wait is real, but it does not have to be empty.

This post is general information. The systems for assessment and funding vary by country and region, so use the parts that fit your situation.

Do not wait to presume competence

A common myth is that a person must prove they are “ready” for AAC before they can have it. There is no prerequisite skill, age or test that should stand between a person and a way to communicate. A simple board can start today.

You do not need a formal assessment to:

  • offer choices;
  • model a few words;
  • keep a paper board within reach;
  • respond to gestures, looks and sounds;
  • try a free or low-cost app.

Starting early gives the person practice and gives you information for the assessment.

Keep a simple record

When the appointment comes, examples help. The Working with a speech and language therapist post explains what to bring and what to ask. Collect:

  • how the person currently communicates;
  • what has been tried and what happened;
  • three moments that went well;
  • three situations that are hard;
  • short videos of real interaction, with consent;
  • questions you want to ask.

Do not create a full-time paperwork project. A few notes and one or two videos are enough.

Ask for interim support

Even without a full assessment, you can ask for:

  • a short consultation with a speech and language therapist;
  • advice from an occupational therapist about access;
  • a trial of a paper board or a simple app;
  • support from school or a local AAC team;
  • information about funding options and waiting times.

Put requests in writing and keep a copy. Ask what the next step is and when to expect it.

Use free and low-cost options

A paper board costs almost nothing. Many apps offer a free version or a trial. A simple photo board on a phone can work temporarily. The goal is not the perfect system; it is a way to communicate while you wait.

If you try an app, check:

  • whether it works offline;
  • whether it stores data locally;
  • whether you can export or back up custom words;
  • whether the voice is understandable;
  • whether the person can access the buttons.

Involve the person

If the person can take part, ask what they want to say. A board built around their interests is more likely to be used than a generic one. If they cannot tell you directly, watch what they choose and ask the people who know them well.

Connect with other families

Waiting can feel isolating. Parent groups, disability organisations and AAC communities can share practical experience and local knowledge. They are not a substitute for professional advice, but they can help you feel less alone and learn what to ask for.

Prepare for the appointment

Bring:

  • your notes and videos;
  • the current communication system, including paper copies;
  • a list of questions;
  • the person themselves, where possible;
  • someone who can take notes if you need support.

Ask:

  • What are we trying to achieve first?
  • Which words or access method should we prioritise?
  • What can we do at home before the next appointment?
  • What signs would suggest we need a different approach?
  • When will we review this?

When to chase

If a waiting time has passed, or the person’s needs have changed significantly, follow up. A short, polite message asking for an update and explaining the impact is reasonable. Keep a record of dates and responses.

A final note

Waiting for support is not the same as doing nothing. A paper board, a few modelled words and a responsive partner can make a difference now. The assessment can refine the system later.
