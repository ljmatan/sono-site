Friendships and playdates: AAC with children outside the family

Friends usually adapt quickly when they know what to do. Simple preparations help a child with AAC join in, be understood and stay in charge of their own voice.

Cet article n'est pas encore traduit dans votre langue ; il est donc affiché en anglais. Aidez-nous à le traduire

Pas un avis médical. Cet article partage des informations générales et des expériences du quotidien. Il ne constitue pas un avis médical, thérapeutique ou diagnostique. Pour toute décision concernant votre enfant, parlez-en à son médecin, à son orthophoniste ou à un autre professionnel qualifié.

Friendships are built in ordinary moments: playing a game, sharing a snack, laughing at the same silly thing. AAC should be part of those moments, not a barrier to them. Most children are curious and adaptable when an adult gives them a simple explanation.

The aim is not to make a friend into a therapist. It is to create a playdate where both children can be children.

Prepare the friend, briefly

Before the playdate, tell the friend’s parent or the friend, in plain language:

“Alex uses this tablet to talk. You can talk to Alex normally. If you don’t understand, wait, or ask Alex to show you again.”

Keep it short. A long explanation can make the device feel more complicated than it is. Answer questions if they come.

Choose a good first activity

Pick something with built-in turns and a clear reason to communicate:

  • a board game;
  • building something together;
  • playing with cars or dolls;
  • cooking or decorating biscuits;
  • a treasure hunt;
  • a simple video game with turns.

Avoid activities that depend on fast spoken negotiation or a lot of reading. The goal is shared fun, not a test of communication. The Play, pause and sabotage post has low-pressure game ideas.

Model with the friend

Children learn by copying. If you model a word during play, the friend may copy you:

“MORE? Yes, more bricks.”

“Your turn. MY TURN.”

Do not ask the friend to teach or prompt the child who uses AAC. That turns a friendship into a job. Let the friend participate naturally.

Do not make the friend an interpreter

Some friends understand the person’s speech or symbols quickly. That is lovely, but it can become a burden. If the friend translates every message, the child who uses AAC has less practice speaking for themselves.

You can say: “Let Alex answer this one.” Then wait. The friend can still help if something is genuinely unclear.

Prepare the home

If the playdate is at your home, put the device or paper board where the children will play. Charge it beforehand. Have a backup in the room. Remove obvious hazards if the device might get knocked over.

If the playdate is elsewhere, bring the device, a charger and a paper copy. Tell the other parent where the board is and how to use it, briefly.

Let them be silly

Humour is a social skill. Add words such as SILLY, YUCK, BOO, FUNNY and AGAIN. A board that can make a friend laugh is a board that gets used.

Do not correct every mismatch or every random tap during play. Respond to the message, keep the game going and model the word you think fits.

Handle the hard parts

Friendships can include conflict. A child with AAC needs words for NO, STOP, MY TURN, NOT and HELP. Model them during play, and support the child to use them when something goes wrong.

If a friend is unkind about the device, address it with the friend and their parent. A calm, firm “The device is how Alex talks; it stays” is usually enough. If it continues, reconsider the playdate.

Talk about privacy

A friend may see photos or messages on the device. Explain that the device belongs to the person and that some things are private. If needed, use a caretaker lock for settings. For older children and teenagers, privacy matters even more.

A small plan

This week, invite one friend for a short, structured activity. Prepare the friend with one sentence. Put the board in the middle of the play. Model two words. Let the children lead. End while it is still fun.

Friendships do not require a perfect communication system. They require repetition, patience and a child who is allowed to be themselves.
