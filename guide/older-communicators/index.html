Usage guide

Teenagers and adults

How to set Sono up for a teenager or an adult in a way that respects their age, their privacy and their right to decide, and what to know when speech is lost later in life. Every person is different, and adults who have lost speech through illness or injury should be assessed by a speech and language therapist.

Not medical advice. This guide shares general information and everyday experience. It is not medical, therapeutic or diagnostic advice. For decisions about your child, talk to their doctor, speech and language therapist or another qualified professional.

On this page

  1. Words and pictures that fit their age
  2. Their board, their voice: ask first
  3. Assume they understand more than they can show

  5. What Sono offers adults, and its limits

Words and pictures that fit their age # — Copy link to this section

Teenagers and adults deserve a board that sounds and looks like them. ASHA, the American Speech-Language-Hearing Association, says the words on a communication system should be in line with those of the person's peers and reflect their language, culture and identity, and that personal, meaningful words make it less likely that the system ends up unused. ASHA also names photographs, along with written words, as symbols that are easy to guess.

How to do it in Sono

  1. Ask the person, and the people who know them best, what they want to talk about: friends, music, sport, cooking, work, their phone, what's on television.
  2. Make symbols for their life under Caretaker Settings > My Symbols: tap +, add a photo of their own mug, bus stop, team shirt or best friend, give it a name and tap Create.
  3. Swap built-in pictures for photos under Caretaker Settings > Customise Symbols: tap a symbol, then Choose Photo or Take Photo.
  4. Together with them, hide what doesn't fit under Caretaker Settings > Hidden Symbols, for example TOY, DOLL or TEDDY BEAR, and switch off whole categories they don't need under Caretaker Settings > Categories. Nothing is lost: everything can be brought back later.
  5. Under Caretaker Settings > Personalisation, choose the picture style of the people illustrations (Neutral, Male or Female), and let them pick the accent colour and a light or dark appearance. Under Board Display, Display Style offers Card or Circle tiles, and Grid Size sets how big they are.
  6. Model the words people their age use, about the things they care about: COFFEE, MUSIC, PHONE, FRIEND.

Sources

  • ASHA — Augmentative and Alternative Communication (Practice Portal)

Their board, their voice: ask first # — Copy link to this section

For someone who communicates with Sono, the board is their voice, and Message History is a record of what they said. ISAAC, the International Society for Augmentative and Alternative Communication, says a person's own control over their communication should be central. AssistiveWare, which makes communication apps, advises asking before you look at, touch or use someone's device, and notes that some people prefer you to use your own. Alyssa Hillary Zisk, who uses a communication aid and now leads research at AssistiveWare, writes that using one should not mean having less privacy or independence than anyone else. An article for practitioners adds that people should be told what a device records and be able to agree or refuse.

How to do it in Sono

  1. Ask before you change their board, and show them what changed: hidden symbols, new photos, recordings, the layout, the Grid Size.
  2. If they prefer, model on a second device instead of theirs. Sono is free to install on as many devices as you like, and with their agreement Caretaker Settings > Backup & Restore copies their symbols and settings across.
  3. Tell them about Message History: it keeps the last 20 sentences spoken from the strip, and Recents the last 30 symbols used. Agree who may look. Clear History and Clear Recents are under Caretaker Settings > Message History.
  4. There is no account and no cloud: their symbols, photos, recordings and history stay on the device. One note: the device's own voice may send the spoken text to the device maker's servers, and Does the app work offline? explains how to avoid that.
  5. If they can, let them manage their own board in Caretaker Settings. The short addition question behind the gear is there to keep young children out, not them.
  6. Pin App to Screen, under Kiosk Mode, keeps the device in Sono. On their own phone, ask first.

Sources

  • ISAAC — What is AAC?
  • AssistiveWare — How to be a respectful communication partner
  • AssistiveWare (Zisk, 2019, updated 2023) — Respecting privacy in AAC
  • PrAACtical AAC (Zangari, 2017) — Privacy and AAC: a few reflections

Assume they understand more than they can show # — Copy link to this section

Not being able to speak tells you little for certain about what a person understands. In 1984 Anne Donnellan, a researcher in special education, described the "least dangerous assumption": when we can't know for sure, we should act on the assumption that does the least harm if it turns out to be wrong. As Cheryl Jorgensen, a researcher in inclusive education, explains, that means assuming the person can understand and learn, and questioning the teaching before questioning the person. AssistiveWare, which makes communication apps, puts it this way: it is far more dangerous to doubt the person and do nothing than to believe in them and start, and nobody is too young or too old to begin.

What to do

  1. Talk to the person, not about them, also in front of doctors and visitors. The Communication Bill of Rights, drawn up by a joint committee of US professional associations, includes the right to be addressed directly and not to be talked about as if not there.
  2. Explain what is happening and why, even when they can't answer yet.
  3. Offer real choices and accept the answer, including MAYBE and NO THANKS.
  4. Give them the whole board, not only requests. For example:
    • Questions: WHAT, WHY, WHERE, WHO
    • Opinions: GOOD, BAD, FUNNY
    • Answers: YES, NO, I DON'T KNOW
  5. Leave ABCs and 123s on the board if there is any chance they read or count. If they can type, Read Aloud in the Activities tab speaks whatever they type, and in the Advanced and Menu layouts Search finds a symbol from part of its name.
  6. Don't test them. Give them time, wait, and let them show you.

Sources

  • Jorgensen (2005) — The least dangerous assumption: a challenge to create a new paradigm. Disability Solutions 6(3) (PDF)
  • AssistiveWare — Presuming competence
  • NJC / ASHA — Communication Bill of Rights, 3rd edition

After a stroke, or with a progressive condition # — Copy link to this section

Adults who lose speech through a stroke, a brain injury or a progressive condition such as motor neurone disease need a specialist assessment, not just an app. The Royal College of Speech and Language Therapists, the UK professional body, says communication aids should be introduced at the right time for each person: with a progressive condition, perhaps while speech is still barely affected, and with care, because news of losing speech is hard to take. NICE, the UK's National Institute for Health and Care Excellence, asks for a speech and language therapist's assessment when communication is severely affected after a stroke, and without delay for motor neurone disease. After a stroke, aphasia, which NICE describes as a loss or impairment of the ability to use and understand language, can affect understanding too, so a therapist should judge what will help. ASHA, the American Speech-Language-Hearing Association, lists the feeling that a communication aid is a sign of the illness progressing among the barriers to using one, so go at the person's pace.

What to do

  1. Ask the stroke team, neurologist or family doctor for a speech and language therapy assessment, and tell the therapist about Sono.
  2. Use every way of communicating. NICE recommends training the people who talk with someone who has aphasia to slow down, not interrupt and use props, gestures and drawing. In the Advanced and Menu layouts, Draw on the sentence strip opens a drawing board.
  3. While speech is still clear, ask the therapist about voice banking (building a computer voice that sounds like them) and message banking (recording words and phrases in their own voice). The Royal College says both are best done before speech changes much. In Sono, they can record their own voice for chosen symbols: Caretaker Settings > Customise Symbols, tap a symbol, then Record voice. Backup & Restore keeps a copy.
  4. Review the setup with the therapist as things change.

Sources

  • RCSLT (2024) — Position statement: augmentative and alternative communication (PDF)
  • NICE (2023) — Stroke rehabilitation in adults (NG236)
  • NICE (2016, updated 2019) — Motor neurone disease: assessment and management (NG42)
  • RCSLT — Voice banking: clinical information for SLTs
  • ASHA — Augmentative and Alternative Communication (Practice Portal)

What Sono offers adults, and its limits # — Copy link to this section

Sono is a free picture board for everyday communication, used mainly by touch. It can be one tool among several, but it isn't a complete communication system for every adult. It's worth knowing where it stops:

  • No switch scanning (pressing one large button while the choices light up one by one) and no eye-gaze control (choosing by looking at the screen). A keyboard or a stylus helps some people who find tapping hard, but not everyone (see Tapping and coordination).
  • Typing whole sentences to be spoken is only possible in Read Aloud, in the Activities tab, not on the board itself.
  • No account and no cloud: nothing follows the person to another device unless you use Backup & Restore.
  • Not for emergencies, and not a healthcare service (see Is Sono a therapy?).

The Royal College of Speech and Language Therapists says every kind of system, from paper boards to dedicated devices, should be on offer, with a trial before anything is recommended or bought and regular reviews as needs change. NICE, the UK's National Institute for Health and Care Excellence, says in its guidance on motor neurone disease that paper alphabet, word or picture boards and tablet-based voice output may both help, and that specialist services should be involved when eye-gaze control is needed.

What to do

  1. Use Sono for what it does well: quick words, familiar photos and recorded voices.
  2. Keep a paper backup: Caretaker Settings > Print Boards, then Share PDF. Printed ABCs can be used for pointing at letters.
  3. If tapping gets harder, try fewer columns or the Menu layout, and ask an occupational therapist about other ways to reach and use a device.
  4. Tell the therapist and the team what you use, so that Sono fits in with what they recommend.

Sources

  • RCSLT (2024) — Position statement: augmentative and alternative communication (PDF)
  • NICE (2016, updated 2019) — Motor neurone disease: assessment and management (NG42)
