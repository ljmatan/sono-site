Choosing words that sound like the person

A board should fit a real life, not a generic list. How to choose vocabulary around interests, age, culture and the words a person actually needs.

Ovaj članak još nije preveden na tvoj jezik pa je prikazan na engleskom. Pomozi nam ga prevesti

Nije medicinski savjet. Ovaj članak donosi opće informacije i iskustva iz svakodnevice. Nije medicinski, terapijski ni dijagnostički savjet. O odlukama koje se tiču vašeg djeteta razgovarajte s liječnikom, logopedom ili drugim kvalificiranim stručnjakom.

A symbol board can be technically well organised and still feel like it belongs to someone else. The words are the difference. If the vocabulary does not fit the person’s age, interests, family and daily life, the board becomes a task rather than a voice.

Choosing vocabulary is not a one-off job. It changes as the person grows, changes schools, discovers new interests or needs new ways to be independent.

Start with the person, not the diagnosis

Two people with the same diagnosis can need very different words. Ask:

  • What does this person love, hate, ask for and complain about?
  • Who are the important people in their life?
  • What do they do on a normal Tuesday?
  • What do they need to say at school, at home, at work or in the community?
  • What makes them laugh?
  • What do they want to be called, and what names do they use for other people?

If the person can answer, ask them directly. If they cannot, ask the people who know them well, and watch what they choose, reach for and avoid.

Include more than nouns

A board full of objects can name the world but cannot run a conversation. Balance the vocabulary:

  • Core words: I, you, want, more, stop, go, help, like, not, all done.
  • People: family names, friends, staff, pets.
  • Actions: eat, drink, play, open, close, wash, go, stop.
  • Feelings: happy, sad, tired, angry, scared, excited.
  • Social words: hi, bye, please, thank you, sorry, my turn.
  • Personal words: a favourite show, a place, a food, a private joke.

The exact mix depends on the person. The point is to avoid a board that only works as a menu.

Match age and identity

A teenager may not want cartoon pictures designed for a preschooler. An adult may not want a board that feels childish. Ask about:

  • symbol style and photo vs illustration;
  • the voice: accent, age, gender, or a familiar recorded voice;
  • the words used for identity, family and relationships;
  • the topics that matter: music, sport, work, dating, politics, religion.

Respectful vocabulary is not a cosmetic detail. It affects whether someone wants to use the board at all.

Include home language and dialect

If the family speaks more than one language, the board should reflect the language used at home, or both languages if that is how the person communicates. Names, foods and family words may not have a neat translation. Keep the words the person actually uses, not a standard version of the language.

Review without churning

A board should be stable enough to learn but flexible enough to grow. A useful rhythm is:

  • add a few words when a new interest or routine appears;
  • hide words that are not needed yet rather than deleting them;
  • keep core words in the same place;
  • review every few months, or when something changes at school, work or home.

Do not rebuild the whole board every week. The person is learning where things are.

Ask for help with the hard choices

A speech and language therapist can help with vocabulary selection, especially when the person has complex needs or uses a different communication system. An occupational therapist can help if access is difficult. The person themselves, and the people who know them best, are the most important experts.
