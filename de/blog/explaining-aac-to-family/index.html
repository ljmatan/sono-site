Explaining AAC to grandparents and relatives without a lecture

A one-sentence explanation, three words to model and permission to get it wrong can turn a well-meaning relative into a confident communication partner.

Family gatherings can be wonderful and complicated. Grandparents, aunts, uncles and family friends want to connect with the person who uses AAC, but they may not know what to do. Some talk only to the parent. Some treat the device as a toy. Some ask, “But will they ever speak?”

You do not need to give a lecture. A few simple agreements can change the visit.

Start with one sentence

Keep the first explanation short enough to remember:

“This is how Alex talks. The board speaks for Alex. You can talk to Alex normally.”

Then add only what the person needs for the next hour: where the key words are, and how long to wait. Details can come later.

Choose three words

Before a visit, pick three words that fit the occasion: HELLO, MORE, LOOK, ALL DONE, HELP, or a favourite food. Show the relative where those words are on the board. Ask them to model one during the visit, not quiz.

Three words are manageable. Twenty words are a homework assignment.

Give them a script for waiting

Most relatives fill silence because they are anxious, not because they are unkind. A simple script helps:

  • Say one short sentence.
  • Tap one word.
  • Count to ten silently.
  • Then wait a little longer.

If you can, demonstrate it once. It is much easier to copy than to read about.

Answer the awkward questions

Relatives may ask questions that feel blunt:

  • “Will they ever talk?” “We don’t know. Right now this gives them a way to communicate while we keep supporting speech too.”
  • “Isn’t the tablet a screen?” “It is a voice. Screen time rules are different for a communication aid.”
  • “Why don’t you just make them ask?” “We model instead of testing. Testing makes communication feel like an exam.”
  • “Can’t they just point?” “They can, and we accept that too. The board gives them more options.”

You do not have to educate everyone. A calm answer, repeated as often as needed, is enough.

Protect the person’s dignity

Do not talk about the person as if they are not there. Do not demonstrate the device on them without asking. Do not share private messages or photos to prove a point. If a relative wants to see how it works, ask the person first, or show a neutral example.

This matters especially with adults who use AAC. Their communication is theirs, not a family demonstration.

Let relatives help in small ways

People are more likely to become allies when they have a real job. Give them one:

  • Hold the paper backup.
  • Model a word during a game.
  • Wait for an answer before filling the silence.
  • Ask the person a direct question and then listen.

A small, specific role is better than a general instruction to “be supportive”.

When a relative gets it wrong

Correct the behaviour, not the person. “Actually, it works better if we wait a bit longer” is easier to hear than “You’re doing it wrong.” If someone repeatedly removes the device or quizzes the person, be firm: “The device stays with Alex. It’s their voice.”

You can also send a short message after the visit with one thing to try next time. Most people improve when the feedback is concrete.

A final thought

AAC is new to many people. Curiosity, awkwardness and mistakes are normal. The goal is not a family of experts. It is a family where the person’s voice is expected, noticed and answered, even by the relatives who only visit twice a year.
