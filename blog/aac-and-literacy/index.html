Reading, writing and AAC: symbols are not the enemy of literacy

A communication board and a love of reading can grow together. Practical ways to include letters, sounds and writing without turning every moment into a lesson.

Not medical advice. This article shares general information and everyday experience. It is not medical, therapeutic or diagnostic advice. For decisions about your child, talk to their doctor, speech and language therapist or another qualified professional.

There is an old assumption that a person who uses symbols cannot learn to read or write, or that literacy should wait until communication is “sorted”. Both ideas sell people short. Communication and literacy support each other: reading opens more words, and a way to communicate makes reading and writing more useful.

You do not need a formal programme to start. You need access to letters, meaningful text and the expectation that the person can learn.

Presume competence

The first principle is to presume the person can learn. That does not mean every person will become a fluent reader. It means not withholding letters, books or writing because of an assumption about what is possible.

Provide access to the alphabet, whether through a keyboard, a letter board, an app page or a set of letter cards. Even if the person does not use it yet, the route should exist.

Read together, not just to

Shared reading is one of the easiest places to build literacy. Choose a book the person enjoys, not a reading scheme. Then:

  • point to the words as you read;
  • pause before a repeated line;
  • let the person turn the page;
  • comment on the pictures;
  • model symbols for the key words.

A familiar book read fifty times is not a waste. Repetition builds prediction, and prediction is a reading skill. The Books, songs and repetition post has ideas for choosing books that invite participation.

Make writing useful

Writing is more motivating when it does something. Try:

  • a shopping list the person adds one item to;
  • a card for a relative;
  • a sign for a bedroom door;
  • a message to a friend;
  • a story about a real event;
  • a name on a drawing.

The writing does not have to be neat or conventional. Approximations count. A scribble that stands for a word is an early writing attempt, not a failure.

Connect letters and sounds

Short, playful activities work better than drills:

  • find words that start with the same sound;
  • match magnetic letters to a name;
  • sing an alphabet song;
  • sort pictures by initial sound;
  • write a letter in sand, shaving foam or on a tablet.

If the person uses an AAC system with a keyboard, model typing a word and let them explore. If the system has a “spell” or alphabet page, keep it accessible rather than hidden.

Do not replace symbols with literacy

Symbols are not a beginner’s tool to be abandoned. Many people use symbols and text together for years. A robust system includes core words, fringe vocabulary and, where possible, the alphabet.

Do not remove symbols because a person is “too old” for them. Age-appropriate symbols and text can coexist at any age.

Keep the pressure low

Literacy can become another area where the person is tested. Avoid turning every book into a comprehension quiz or every drawing into a spelling correction. Notice what the person reads and writes, and respond to the meaning first.

If the person is tired, stop. If they choose a book that seems too easy, let them. Pleasure is part of learning.

Ask for help

If the person has a vision, hearing or motor difference, a specialist teacher or occupational therapist can suggest adaptations. A speech and language therapist can help connect AAC and literacy goals. You do not have to design a curriculum alone.

A small plan

This week, put one letter-based activity into a routine the person already enjoys. Read a favourite book and point to the words. Add one letter card to the bath. Write one word together on a shopping list. Keep it short and follow the person’s interest.
