Modelling vs prompting: how to help without taking over

Modelling shows someone how a board works. Prompting asks them to perform. The difference matters, and a few small habits keep help from becoming a test.

Not medical advice. This article shares general information and everyday experience. It is not medical, therapeutic or diagnostic advice. For decisions about your child, talk to their doctor, speech and language therapist or another qualified professional.

You want to help. So you say, “Show me ‘more’,” or “Tap the apple.” The intention is good. The effect can be a quiz, and quizzes make communication feel like work.

Modelling and prompting are both useful, but they do different things. Understanding the difference makes it easier to support someone without taking over their voice.

What modelling is

Modelling means using the communication system yourself while you talk, without asking the person to respond.

“I want a drink.” tap WANT, tap DRINK

“Look, a dog!” tap LOOK, tap DOG

You are not testing. You are showing how the board works, the way a parent speaks around a child who is learning to talk. The person can join in or not.

Modelling:

  • shows where words live;
  • demonstrates sentence building;
  • makes the board feel normal;
  • carries no demand;
  • can be done by anyone, anywhere.

What prompting is

A prompt is a cue for the person to do something. It can be verbal (“show me”), gestural (pointing at a button), or physical (guiding a hand). Prompting is not inherently bad. It can help someone learn a new skill, especially when paired with fading.

The risk is prompt dependency: the person waits for a cue before communicating. If every message begins with “show me”, the person may learn to perform rather than to speak.

Prefer modelling in ordinary moments

Most of the time, modelling is enough. During breakfast, model MORE, ALL DONE and WANT. During play, model GO, AGAIN and STOP. Do not ask the person to repeat. The ten-second pause is the partner skill that goes with modelling.

If they do respond, great. If they do not, keep modelling and move on. The absence of a response is not a failure; it is information about timing, motivation or access.

When a prompt might help

There are moments when a prompt is appropriate:

  • Safety. “Stop” may need an immediate, clear prompt if someone is about to hurt themselves.
  • A new routine. A first trip to the toilet or a new classroom may need a gesture toward the right word.
  • A person who is learning the layout. A light verbal cue can help them find a word they already know.
  • A choice that is genuinely open. “Do you want juice or water?” is a question, not a prompt to perform.

If you prompt, prompt the word, not the person’s worth:

  • “MORE?” tap MORE rather than “Say more. Come on, you know this.”
  • “Do you want help?” rather than “Ask me properly.”

Fade the prompt

If you use a prompt, make it smaller next time:

  1. A full verbal prompt: “Tap MORE.”
  2. A partial prompt: “More?”
  3. A gesture toward the board.
  4. A pause and an expectant look.
  5. Nothing.

Move down the list as the person responds. If they need the full prompt again, that is fine. Learning is not linear.

Watch your own habits

It is easy to slip into prompting all day. A quick self-check:

  • How often do I ask the person to “show me”?
  • Do I wait after modelling, or do I fill the silence?
  • Do I praise every tap as if it were a performance?
  • Do I answer for the person before they have a chance?
  • Do I model words for my own messages, or only for theirs?

None of these questions require a perfect score. Noticing is enough to change one habit.

A small experiment

For one day, replace “show me” with modelling. When you want the person to say something, say it yourself on the board and then wait. Notice what happens. You may find that the person communicates more when the pressure is lower.
