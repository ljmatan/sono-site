Privacy and dignity: AAC in personal care and public places

A communication device holds private words, photos and messages. How to protect dignity, ask before sharing and keep the person in control.

Ten artykuł nie został jeszcze przetłumaczony na twój język, dlatego wyświetlamy go po angielsku. Pomóż nam go przetłumaczyć

To nie jest porada medyczna. Ten artykuł przedstawia ogólne informacje i codzienne doświadczenia. Nie jest poradą medyczną, terapeutyczną ani diagnostyczną. O decyzjach dotyczących twojego dziecka porozmawiaj z jego lekarzem, logopedą lub innym wykwalifikowanym specjalistą.

A communication device is more intimate than most technology. It holds the words a person uses to ask for help, say no, talk to family, or complain about the person supporting them. It may hold photos of their body, their home, their children or their medical needs.

That intimacy deserves the same respect as a diary or a private conversation.

The device belongs to the person

It is easy for a parent or carer to treat the device as equipment: something to set up, update, charge and hand over. But the person using it has a right to privacy. That means:

  • not reading messages or browsing history without a clear reason and, where possible, consent;
  • not sharing screenshots or photos of the board on social media without permission;
  • not using the device to test or demonstrate the person in front of strangers;
  • not deleting the person’s own symbols or messages without asking.

For children, a parent may need to check settings or safety. Do it openly, explain why, and involve the child as much as possible. For adults, assume competence and ask first.

Personal care

Personal care is where dignity is most easily lost. A person may need to say STOP, WAIT, HELP, PRIVATE or I NEED A MINUTE while being washed, dressed or moved. Those words need to be easy to reach, and they need to work.

  • Keep a small board or the device within reach during care, not on a shelf.
  • Agree on a signal for “stop” and respect it whenever it is safe to do so.
  • Explain what you are going to do before you do it.
  • Cover the person where possible; close doors; ask before exposing them.
  • If a procedure cannot wait, say so calmly and explain what will happen.

This is not only about comfort. It is about consent and control.

Public places

A device can attract attention. Strangers may stare, ask questions, or talk to the carer instead of the person. A few phrases can help:

“This is how they communicate. Please ask them.”

“We’re fine, thank you.”

“They can answer for themselves.”

If the person wants to explain, let them. If they do not, you can protect the boundary without being rude. A person should not have to perform their disability to be treated with respect.

Data and settings

Sono stores messages, custom photos and settings on the device. The Privacy Policy explains what is stored and what leaves the device. Practical steps:

  • use the caretaker lock for settings if the person might change them by accident;
  • back up before updating or resetting;
  • decide together what is kept in message history;
  • avoid putting sensitive medical details on a symbol that is visible to everyone;
  • if the device is lost or shared, change any relevant settings and review what was stored.

Sharing stories

Families often share AAC wins online: the first word, a funny sentence, a moment of connection. That can be a lifeline for other families. It can also expose a person who did not choose to be public.

Ask before posting. For a child, ask in a way they can understand, and respect a no. Blur names and faces if needed. The story can still help someone without taking away dignity.

A simple rule

If you would not read it, screenshot it or share it about a speaking adult without asking, do not do it to a person who uses AAC. The device is a voice, and a voice is private.
