AAC at work: meetings, interviews and everyday colleagues

A communication system belongs in the workplace too. Practical ways to prepare for meetings, ask for adjustments and keep a professional voice.

Ovaj članak još nije preveden na tvoj jezik pa je prikazan na engleskom. Pomozi nam ga prevesti

Nije medicinski savjet. Ovaj članak donosi opće informacije i iskustva iz svakodnevice. Nije medicinski, terapijski ni dijagnostički savjet. O odlukama koje se tiču vašeg djeteta razgovarajte s liječnikom, logopedom ili drugim kvalificiranim stručnjakom.

Work is full of communication: briefings, small talk, interviews, disagreements, jokes and quick questions in a corridor. For an adult who uses AAC, the workplace can be both an opportunity for independence and a place where communication barriers are treated as a performance issue.

The principles are the same as anywhere else: presume competence, keep the system available and make the environment meet the person halfway.

The device is work equipment

A communication device should be treated like a laptop or a phone: something the person needs to do the job. It should be:

  • allowed in meetings and on the shop floor;
  • charged and within reach;
  • protected by a case if the environment is physical;
  • included in any reasonable adjustments conversation.

An employer who would not take away a wheelchair should not take away a voice. If a uniform policy or a “no phones” rule creates a problem, ask for an explicit exception for the communication device. The AAC is not only for children post covers adult life more broadly.

Prepare a short work profile

A one-page profile can help colleagues without turning the person into a case study:

  • how the person communicates;
  • how long to wait for an answer;
  • whether they prefer written questions before a meeting;
  • how to reach them in an emergency;
  • what to do if the device is not working;
  • who to contact if there is a problem.

The person should decide what is included. Disclosure is a personal choice; the profile is not an obligation.

Meetings

Meetings are easier when everyone knows the format:

  • share the agenda in advance;
  • circulate slides before the meeting if possible;
  • allow written contributions;
  • give the person time to answer;
  • do not talk over the device or finish its sentences;
  • repeat or rephrase a question if asked.

If the person uses a sentence-building app, a long contribution takes time. Build in pauses rather than filling them. A chair who says “take your time” and means it changes the room.

Interviews

An interview is a high-pressure communication situation. Ask for adjustments in advance:

  • questions in writing;
  • extra time;
  • a familiar communication partner if needed;
  • a quiet room;
  • a break;
  • permission to use the device.

An employer should assess the person’s ability to do the job, not their speed of speech. If an interview process is inaccessible, that is a barrier to equality, not evidence of inability.

Everyday colleagues

Small talk can be the hardest part of work. Colleagues may not know how to chat with someone who uses AAC. A few words from the person or a supportive manager can help:

“You can ask me directly. I’ll answer on here.”

“I like football. Talk to me about that.”

Invite the person to the coffee break, the lunch table and the after-work drink. Do not assume they would not want to come.

Ask for what you need

Reasonable adjustments vary by country and employer, but common requests include:

  • a quieter desk;
  • written instructions;
  • extra time for training;
  • a device stand or mount;
  • captioning for videos;
  • flexible communication methods;
  • an accessible fire alarm or emergency plan.

Put requests in writing where possible. Keep a record of what was agreed.

A final note

A job is not only a source of income. It is structure, relationships, identity and a way to contribute. A communication system that works at work is part of making that possible.
