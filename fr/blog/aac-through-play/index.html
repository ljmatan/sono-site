Play, pause and sabotage: low-pressure ways to model

Games create reasons to communicate without a worksheet. How to use people games, waiting and gentle sabotage to make symbols feel useful.

Cet article n'est pas encore traduit dans votre langue ; il est donc affiché en anglais. Aidez-nous à le traduire

Pas un avis médical. Cet article partage des informations générales et des expériences du quotidien. Il ne constitue pas un avis médical, thérapeutique ou diagnostique. Pour toute décision concernant votre enfant, parlez-en à son médecin, à son orthophoniste ou à un autre professionnel qualifié.

Play is where communication gets practised without anyone calling it practice. A game has turns, surprises, repetition and a reason to say something. It also gives you a natural pause, which is the most useful teaching tool you have.

You do not need a special AAC game. You need a person, a bit of silliness, and a board within reach.

Start with people games

People games are games where the fun comes from you, not a toy: tickling, swinging, bouncing, chasing, peekaboo, spinning, “ready, steady, go”. They are ideal because they are easy to repeat and easy to stop.

Model one or two words:

  • Ready… GO!
  • MORE
  • AGAIN
  • STOP
  • LOOK

Then pause. The pause is the invitation. If the person looks, reaches, vocalises or taps, respond immediately. The game continues because they communicated.

Use gentle sabotage

Sabotage sounds unkind; in AAC practice it usually means creating a small, safe problem that gives the person a reason to communicate.

  • Give a favourite snack in a sealed container.
  • Start a familiar song and stop.
  • Put the puzzle piece just out of reach.
  • Forget the bubbles, then look at the board.
  • Open the book to the wrong page.

Model the word that fits: HELP, OPEN, MORE, AGAIN, LOOK. Then wait. If nothing comes, solve it together and model again. The goal is not to frustrate; it is to make communication useful.

Follow their lead

The best play is the play the person chooses. Watch what they return to: spinning, water, a particular song, a tickle game. That is your vocabulary list. Put the words for that activity close together and model them every time.

If they want to do the same thing for twenty minutes, that is not a problem. Repetition is how language sticks.

Keep the board in the play

A board on the other side of the room is not part of the game. Keep it on the floor, on the sofa, or on the table beside you. If the person is mobile, a paper copy or a phone-sized board can travel with the game.

Do not pause the fun to fetch the device every time. Build the board into the activity from the start.

Model, don’t quiz

During play, comment and participate:

“GO! Down the slide.”

“MORE bubbles? More!”

“Uh oh, it stopped. HELP?”

Avoid turning the game into a test: “Show me GO. Where’s GO? Can you say GO?” The game is the point. The symbols are part of it.

End on a good moment

Stop while it is still fun. A short, successful game is better than a long one that ends in tears. If the person signs “all done”, finish with a clear “ALL DONE” and put the board away with them, not instead of them.

A small plan for this week

Pick one people game and one word. Model that word every time it fits. Keep the board within reach. Notice any response, however small, and respond to it. That is enough.
