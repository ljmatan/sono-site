Build it together: co-designing the board with the person who uses it

A board works better when the person has a say in it. Practical ways to involve children, teenagers and adults in choosing words, images and layout.

Ovaj članak još nije preveden na tvoj jezik pa je prikazan na engleskom. Pomozi nam ga prevesti

Nije medicinski savjet. Ovaj članak donosi opće informacije i iskustva iz svakodnevice. Nije medicinski, terapijski ni dijagnostički savjet. O odlukama koje se tiču vašeg djeteta razgovarajte s liječnikom, logopedom ili drugim kvalificiranim stručnjakom.

A communication board is a personal object. It holds the words a person needs, the people they love, the jokes they tell and the things they want to complain about. When someone else designs it alone, even with the best intentions, it can end up fitting the designer’s idea of the person rather than the person themselves.

Co-designing does not require a formal process. It requires curiosity, patience and a willingness to hand over some control.

Start with the person’s life

Before opening the app, make a list of what matters:

  • Who are the important people?
  • What does a normal week look like?
  • What does the person love, hate, avoid and ask for?
  • What do they need to say at school, work, home or in the community?
  • What makes them laugh?
  • What do they want to be called?

If the person can answer, ask them. If not, ask the people who know them well and watch what they choose, reach for and ignore.

Offer real choices

Co-design is not a single meeting. It is a series of small choices:

  • “Do you want a photo or a drawing for this word?”
  • “Should this word be on the main page or in a folder?”
  • “Which voice sounds more like you?”
  • “Do you want this word at all?”

Offer two or three options at a time. Accept the answer, even if it is not what you expected. If the person chooses the same thing repeatedly, that is a preference, not a failure of imagination. The Choosing words that sound like the person post has more on vocabulary.

Use sorting activities

For people who find open questions hard, sorting can help. Write words or show symbols on cards and ask:

  • “Which of these do you use every day?”
  • “Which of these is private?”
  • “Which one makes you happy?”
  • “Which one can go away?”

The person can point, sort, or use their AAC system to answer. The activity itself is communication.

Keep core words stable

Co-design does not mean rebuilding the board every week. Core words should stay in predictable places so the person can build motor memory. Change the fringe vocabulary, the photos, the colours and the temporary categories, but protect the layout of the words used most often.

If a change is necessary, explain it and give the person time to adjust.

Design for the person’s body

A board that looks good on a screen may be hard to use. Consider:

  • button size and spacing;
  • contrast and symbol style;
  • reach and mounting;
  • screen angle;
  • glare;
  • whether the person uses a finger, fist, stylus, switch or eye gaze.

An occupational therapist can help with access. The person’s own experience is the most important evidence.

Include the person in the paper copy

If you make a paper backup, show it to the person. Ask whether the words are in the right place. Let them decorate it if they want. A board that looks like theirs is more likely to be used.

Review together

Every few months, look at the board together. Ask:

  • What words are missing?
  • What words are never used?
  • What has changed at school, work or home?
  • What would make the board easier to use?

Do not turn the review into a test of what the person knows. It is a design conversation, not an assessment.

A final thought

Co-design is not a one-off workshop. It is a habit of asking, watching and adjusting. The person who uses the board is the expert on their own life. The rest of us are there to help build the tool.
