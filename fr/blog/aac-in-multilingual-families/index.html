AAC in multilingual families: keep the home language close

A communication board should not flatten a family into one language. How to make room for home language, names, food and the words that feel like home.

Pas un avis médical. Cet article partage des informations générales et des expériences du quotidien. Il ne constitue pas un avis médical, thérapeutique ou diagnostique. Pour toute décision concernant votre enfant, parlez-en à son médecin, à son orthophoniste ou à un autre professionnel qualifié.

Many families speak more than one language at home. A child may hear one language from their parents, another at school, and a third from grandparents. When AAC is introduced, there is sometimes pressure to choose one language and drop the others, usually the school language winning.

That advice deserves a second look. Language is identity, family and belonging. A board that cannot say the words used at home can feel like a stranger in the house.

Start with the language of the heart

The language a person hears most often, and the one used in emotional moments, is a good place to start. For many families that is the home language, not the school language. If a person can communicate about food, comfort, family and feelings in the language that feels natural, they are more likely to use the system.

If the family is bilingual, the board can include both languages. It does not have to be perfectly symmetrical. Start with the words that matter most in each language and add gradually.

Names, food and family words

Some words do not translate neatly: a grandmother’s name, a particular dish, a family joke, a word for a sibling. Keep them as they are. A board that uses the family’s real words feels personal, not generic.

Record familiar voices for these words if the app allows it. A grandparent saying a family word can carry more meaning than a synthetic voice. The Choosing words that sound like the person post has more on vocabulary that fits a real life.

School and home can differ

The school may use a different language, a different symbol set, or a different communication system. That is not automatically a problem. What matters is that the people in both places know which words are being modelled and how to wait.

Ask the school to share their key words with you. Share yours with them. A simple two-column list on the fridge, or a photo of the board, helps everyone stay consistent without pretending the two settings are identical.

Do not wait for one language to be “finished”

A common worry is that two languages will confuse a person who is already finding communication hard. Professionals who work with multilingual families generally recommend supporting the family’s languages rather than removing one. A communication system should add options, not subtract a relationship.

If you are unsure, ask a speech and language therapist who has experience with multilingual learners. If you cannot find one, ask the therapist you have to help you think it through rather than assuming one language must go.

Interpreters and appointments

If appointments happen in a language you are less confident in, ask for an interpreter. Do not rely on a child to interpret medical or educational information. An interpreter should be booked in advance when possible, and the person who uses AAC should be spoken to directly, not only about.

A small family language plan

Write down:

  • the languages used at home, and by whom;
  • five words that matter in each language;
  • the words for family members and favourite foods;
  • who will model which words;
  • what the school uses.

Keep it short. Review it when something changes: a new school, a new carer, a new living arrangement.

The goal is not a perfect bilingual board

It is a board that lets the person say the things that matter in the language that feels like home, and gives them a way into the wider world. Both can be true at once.
