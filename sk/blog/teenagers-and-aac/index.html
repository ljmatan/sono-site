Teenagers and AAC: privacy, independence and a voice of their own

Adolescence changes everything, including communication. How to involve a teenager in choosing their vocabulary, voice and boundaries without hovering.

Tento článok zatiaľ nie je preložený do vášho jazyka, preto sa zobrazuje v angličtine. Pomôžte nám ho preložiť

Nejde o lekársku radu. Tento článok prináša všeobecné informácie a skúsenosti z bežného života. Nejde o lekársku, terapeutickú ani diagnostickú radu. O rozhodnutiach týkajúcich sa vášho dieťaťa sa poraďte s jeho lekárom, logopédom alebo iným kvalifikovaným odborníkom.

A teenager who uses AAC is a teenager first. They want privacy, friends, independence, a sense of style and a say in their own life. A communication system that was designed for a younger child may no longer fit, and continuing to manage it like a child’s device can turn a voice into a source of embarrassment.

The task of adolescence is separation, and AAC has to make room for it.

Hand over the controls

As much as possible, the teenager should decide:

  • which voice the device uses;
  • which symbols or photos are on the board;
  • who can see their messages;
  • when and where they use the device;
  • what they want to be called;
  • which words are private.

This does not mean abandoning support. It means moving from doing things for the teenager to doing them with the teenager, and then stepping back.

Make the vocabulary age-appropriate

A board filled with cartoon animals and preschool songs will not feel like a teenager’s voice. Review it together:

  • music, sport, games, school subjects;
  • friends, relationships, identity;
  • money, travel, work, plans;
  • feelings, opinions, humour;
  • words for consent and boundaries;
  • the language they actually use with friends.

Keep core words stable, but update the fringe vocabulary. Add slang if the teenager wants it. A board that sounds like them is more likely to be used.

Privacy is not optional

A device can hold messages, photos, search history and personal information. For a teenager, this is private. Ask before reading messages. Do not share screenshots or stories online without consent. Do not demonstrate the device to relatives without asking.

If you need to check something for safety, say so and explain why. Do it openly. A teenager who feels surveilled may stop communicating on the device altogether. The Privacy and dignity post covers personal care and public places.

Prepare for the outside world

Teenagers go places without their parents: school, friends’ houses, clubs, public transport. Make sure they can:

  • ask for help;
  • say stop or no;
  • explain that the device is their voice;
  • contact a trusted adult;
  • use a paper backup if the device fails.

Practise short phrases. A keyring card or a phone-sized board can be less conspicuous than a full device in some situations.

Friends and dating

Friendships and romantic relationships are part of adolescence. The teenager may want to talk about crushes, consent, boundaries and identity. Make sure the vocabulary exists, and that the people around them treat them as a sexual being with rights, not a child to be protected from the topic.

If a teenager asks a question you cannot answer, help them find a trusted adult or reliable information. Do not shut the conversation down.

School and transition planning

Secondary school brings new staff, new routines and more complex social situations. A one-page profile can help, but the teenager should have a say in what it says. Transition planning to adult services should start early, with the teenager in the room.

Goals should be theirs, not only the adults’. “I want to order my own drink” or “I want to text my friend” may matter more than a therapy target.

When to step in

Support is still needed. Step in if:

  • the device is being taken or damaged;
  • the teenager is being bullied;
  • their health or safety is at risk;
  • they ask for help.

Step back when the decision is theirs to make, even if you would choose differently. Independence is built through practice, including mistakes.

A small conversation

This week, ask the teenager one question: “What would make your device feel more like yours?” Listen without defending the current setup. Change one thing they ask for. That is a good place to start.
