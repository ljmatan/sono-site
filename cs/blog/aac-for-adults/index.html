AAC is not only for children: adults after stroke and with changing needs

Adults use AAC too, sometimes after a stroke, an injury or a progressive condition. Respect, fatigue and a voice that fits an adult life matter.

Tento článek zatím není přeložen do vašeho jazyka, proto se zobrazuje v angličtině. Pomozte nám ho přeložit

Nejde o lékařskou radu. Tento článek sdílí obecné informace a zkušenosti z běžného života. Nejde o lékařskou, terapeutickou ani diagnostickou radu. O rozhodnutích týkajících se vašeho dítěte se poraďte s jeho lékařem, logopedem nebo jiným kvalifikovaným odborníkem.

AAC is often pictured as a child’s tablet with cartoon symbols. That picture leaves out a lot of people: adults after a stroke, people with motor neurone disease, people with a progressive condition, autistic adults, people with a temporary injury, and older adults who have lost speech.

The principles of good communication do not change with age. The vocabulary, the pace, the privacy and the amount of control absolutely do.

A person, not a patient

An adult who needs AAC is still an adult. They have a history, opinions, relationships, work, preferences and a right to make decisions. A communication board that begins with “juice” and “toilet” can feel like a regression if the person wants to talk about politics, their children or last night’s football match.

Start with the vocabulary of their life:

  • names of family, friends and colleagues;
  • work or volunteering;
  • interests, news, sport, music, religion;
  • feelings and opinions;
  • medical needs and appointments;
  • words for asking, refusing and negotiating;
  • the language they used before, including dialect and swearing if that is part of who they are.

Fatigue is real

After a stroke or during a progressive illness, communication can be exhausting. A long conversation may be possible in the morning and impossible by afternoon. The device should be available for short messages, not only for full sentences. A single word or a look can be enough.

Plan important conversations for the best time of day. Offer breaks. Do not fill every silence. If the person is tired, accept a shorter answer and come back later.

Changing needs

A progressive condition may affect movement, vision, breath or stamina over time. A system that works today may need adjusting in six months. Review access regularly: switch access, eye gaze, larger buttons, a different mount, a quieter environment. An occupational therapist and a speech and language therapist can help plan ahead.

Do not wait for a crisis to think about the next step. A backup method (paper, a second device, a familiar gesture) is part of planning.

Consent and control

Adults have the right to refuse, change their mind and keep private information private. This includes:

  • choosing who sees their messages;
  • deciding whether to use the device in a meeting or at a family gathering;
  • saying no to a communication goal;
  • controlling their own medical information.

If a carer or family member is used to speaking for the person, step back where possible. Ask the person directly. Wait. Let them answer.

Work and community

AAC can support work, volunteering, education and social life. A few adjustments often help: a quiet room for meetings, written agendas in advance, a way to ask for a repeat, and colleagues who know how to wait. An employer or community group may need a short explanation, not a medical history.

A note on identity

Some adults embrace AAC; some feel ambivalent; some are angry that they need it. All of those responses are valid. The aim is not to force gratitude. It is to make a tool that fits the person’s life and gives them more control, not less.
